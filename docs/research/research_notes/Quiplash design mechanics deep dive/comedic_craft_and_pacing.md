# Quiplash Comedic Craft and Pacing: Why It Is Funny and Feels Great

Scope note: These notes cover the "why it works" layer of Quiplash: prompt writing, the head-to-head structure, reveal pacing, the host, screen and phone UX, audiovisual style, and failure modes. The aim is to give a designer enough to rebuild the same feel in an original medical-humor party game. Primary sources from Jackbox staff are quoted wherever they could be retrieved.

## 1. Prompt writing: principles Jackbox writers use

### Takeaway
Jackbox's own published guidance asks for five things:
- A prompt that produces "a lot of quick ideas."
- A writer who can answer their own prompt.
- Specific but not overloaded detail.
- References everyone in the room shares.
- Varied structures, not only fill-in-the-blank.

A second staff post adds tone-setting tactics: funny words, absurd premises, embarrassing personal experiences, and questions the writer genuinely wants answered. The CEO's prototype test, "how fast do you get to laughter?", is the master criterion.

### Cited Findings
**Spencer Ham (Jackbox Games blog, 2 Nov 2016), "How to Write a Good Quiplash Prompt"** ([source](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt))
- **Think ahead.** "If you can't think of a good answer to your own prompt, then it's probably not going to be a very good [one]." Also: "Make sure your prompt lends itself to a lot of quick ideas."
- **Know your audience.** "If you're alienating even 1-2 people, the game is a lot less fun." Inside jokes are fine only for a group that shares them. Example: "Why mom won't let anybody else make the green bean casserole."
- **Be specific, but not excessively so.** "Be specific whenever possible." Ham gives a three-step example:
  - Vague: "The title of an erotic movie"
  - Good: "The title of an erotic movie for elephants"
  - Overloaded: "The title of a 1940's erotic film for elephants at the St. Louis Zoo"
- **Vary structure and joke type.** "Don't just write fill-in-the blanks--mix it up with prompts in the form of questions and statements." Ask for different kinds of answer (phrases, objects, questions), not only puns.
- **Don't overthink it.** "Spend a little bit of time on how you want to frame your idea, but don't labor over it."

**C.J. Tuor (Jackbox Games blog, 14 May 2019), "The Science of Creating a Quiplash Prompt"** ([source](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/the-science-of-creating-a-quiplash-prompt))
- **Start with funny words.** Silly phrases, imaginary characters and absurd premises signal that the answer should be playful. The goal is for players to understand that "their response should be equally whimsical." Example: "What the boogeyman is afraid of."
- **Reliving past mistakes.** "Like all writers, the staff at Jackbox Games are tortured individuals who transform our pain into art." Example: "The worst song when stripping for a lover."
- **Broad audience by default.** "A good Quiplash prompt should work with a variety of audiences." Event-specific prompts are the exception. Example from a Bachelorette Party edition: "Best thing to scream out of a moving party bus."
- **Butt jokes.** Tuor presents these as an observed house pattern, not a tip: "We refuse to say we're sorry." Example: "Rename any famous work of literature so that it is ruined by the word 'butt'."
- **Sincere curiosity.** "We writers may just pose questions and premises we sincerely want to know the answers to." Example: "What are mannequins always thinking?"
- Tuor also notes that "Jackbox Games doesn't record what you type in." The company does not mine player answers to tune prompts.

**Mike Bilder (Jackbox CEO), Nintendo Life interview** ([source](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs))
- Every prototype is judged by "how fast do you get to laughter?"
- "With Quiplash, it was instantaneous. Time to laughter is like zero."
- "Every time we played it, it was funny."
- Quiplash "needed almost no explanation on how to play" and "naturally became the most accessible, quickest to laughs, easiest kind of prototype of the game that we made."

**Brooke Breit (Jackbox creative director), WBUR/WFDD, 23 Oct 2025** ([source](https://www.wfdd.org/2025-10-23/how-chicagos-famous-second-city-comedy-club-fueled-blockbuster-party-game-jackbox))
- Her improv framing of what a prompt is for:
  - "One of the key elements that I always held on to was the idea of making your scene partner look good."
  - "We're just kind of giving you the diving board so that you can jump in."
  - "We're genuinely always trying to follow where the laughs are."
- Room Escape Artist's podcast episode with Breit carries the pull quote "We set players up to be the stars of the show." ([source](https://roomescapeartist.com/2024/10/22/comedy-collaboration-brooke-breit-creative-director-jackbox-games-repod-s8e4/))

**Other prompt sources**
- In-game "suggestion" prompts (here, Tee K.O.'s, a sibling Jackbox game) can fail when they are too generic. One student critique says suggestions like "a romantic person" were never picked because "neither really made sense" in the group's context, which pushed players back to inside jokes. ([Mechanics of Magic, Stanford CS247G, 11 Apr 2025](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2025/04/11/critplay1-amaru/))
- Quiplash 2 supports custom prompts (via episodes), but they cannot be used in round three, per search-result snippets of fan forums. Treat this as secondary. ([Jackbox Fandom list of prompts](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series)/List_of_Prompts); [gameshowforum thread](https://www.gameshowforum.org/index.php?topic=30479.0))

**Prompt archetypes in the staff posts (verbatim examples)**

| Archetype | Example |
|---|---|
| "The title of a ___ for [unexpected audience]" | "The title of an erotic movie for elephants" |
| "The worst ___ when/for [situation]" | "The worst song when stripping for a lover"; "The worst way to spell Mississippi" |
| "Best thing to [do] [in vivid situation]" | "Best thing to scream out of a moving party bus" |
| "What [non-human / mythical thing] is thinking or afraid of" | "What the boogeyman is afraid of"; "What are mannequins always thinking?" |
| "Rename [famous thing] so it is ruined by [word]" | the "butt" literature prompt |
| Inside-joke "Why ___" (custom only) | "Why mom won't let anybody else make the green bean casserole" |

### Inferences
- The "specific, but not excessively so" rule works as a dial. One concrete twist (the "for elephants" part) narrows the comedic space enough to spark ideas. A second or third detail turns it into a puzzle and kills speed.
- For a medical game this translates directly:
  - Vague: "A rejected name for a medical device"
  - Good: "A rejected name for a pediatric enema"
  - Overloaded: "A rejected name for a 1970s pediatric enema sold only in Ohio"
- Spencer Ham's audience rule is the central risk for a medical-humor game. Prompts that need specialty-specific knowledge (for example, an obscure nephrology eponym) will lock out dental or PA students in mixed rooms. That argues for tagging prompts by audience breadth: universal clinical life (night shift, pagers, patients, attendings, cafeteria food, scrubs, board exams), discipline-specific, and specialty-deep. Mixed groups would default to the universal tier.
- The "reliving past mistakes" and "sincere curiosity" tactics fit clinical training well. Shared indignities (pimping on rounds, the first code, charting at 3am) are relatable pain that crosses disciplines.
- "Time to laughter" is a usable playtest metric: measure time from first prompt seen to first group laugh in a fresh session.

## 2. Head-to-head 1v1 design: why two anonymous answers and a vote create tension and laughs

### Takeaway
Each prompt goes to exactly two players, and everyone else votes. That gives every joke a direct comparison (a built-in "versus" punchline) while keeping authors anonymous until the reveal. The anonymity is described as a "moment of safety" that lowers the social risk of trying a joke. The impersonal vote is said to stop people ganging up on winners.

### Cited Findings
- **Core structure.** In rounds 1 and 2, "each player gets two prompts" and "each prompt goes to two randomly chosen players." The other players and the audience vote for the funnier answer, and points scale with votes. ([Wikipedia: Quiplash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiplash))
- **Official framing:** "Players enter a head-to-head battle of the wits by answering a comedic prompt," "letting the other players (and the audience) decide which they like best!" ([Jackbox: Quiplash 3](https://www.jackboxgames.com/quiplash-three)). The original Quiplash page also says there are "no rules or correct answers" and tells players to trust their gut ([Jackbox: Quiplash](https://jackboxgames.com/quiplash), via search snippet).
- **Quiplash bonus.** An answer that wins every other player's vote (and a majority of the audience, if present) earns a "quiplash" and bonus points. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiplash))
- **Jinx.** If both players submit the same answer, they "get a 'jinx' and no points for that prompt." ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiplash))
- **Anonymity as safety.** One analysis says the multi-device setup gives players "a moment of safety where their identity isn't known," letting the room "get used to the answers before players' names are revealed." It argues the judging is "impersonal," which "prevents people ganging up on winners/taking the competition too seriously." ([Jonathan Kula, Mechanics of Magic, Stanford, 30 Apr 2023](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2023/04/30/critical-play-quiplash-judging-games-jonathan/))
- **Competition serves expression.** The same analysis says competition is there "to encourage players to go even harder with their prompts" and that the core fun is fellowship. ([Kula, 2023](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2023/04/30/critical-play-quiplash-judging-games-jonathan/))
- **Make your partner look good.** Breit's improv principle, "making your scene partner look good," frames the game as setting players up rather than competing with them. ([WBUR/WFDD, 2025](https://www.wfdd.org/2025-10-23/how-chicagos-famous-second-city-comedy-club-fueled-blockbuster-party-game-jackbox))
- **Origin.** Quiplash began as "a simple prototype of taking what was the Fibbage engine and breaking it down into something funnier." In Fibbage, players write fake trivia answers and others vote; Quiplash stripped out the trivia so only the comedy was left. (Bilder, [Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs))

### Inferences
- A matchup has three laugh points:
  1. The prompt sets up an expectation.
  2. Answer A and answer B land as two punchlines, and their contrast is a third (the "versus" joke).
  3. The author reveal is a final punchline, because the room learns who wrote what. A quiet resident writing the filthiest answer is a classic payoff.
- A designer should protect all three: never reveal authors before votes are locked, and give the author reveal its own beat.
- Two answers (not five) keeps comparison cheap. Every voter can hold both in their head, which keeps voting fast.
- The jinx and the quiplash bonus encode two values. Jinx punishes the obvious answer. The unanimous bonus rewards the answer the whole room agrees on. Together they push players away from the first idea everyone has and toward the surprising-but-true answer.
- For a medical game, this protects people with less status. A nursing student can beat an attending anonymously and learn about it at the reveal, which is a real social payoff in a hierarchical culture. Designers should keep author identity hidden until after votes, and consider whether attendings should vote on trainees' answers at all.

### Gaps
- I found no Jackbox staff statement on why they chose exactly two answers per prompt, or on the reveal as a deliberate "second punchline." These are design inferences, supported only by the third-party analysis above.
- I could not verify the exact point values (per-vote points, quiplash bonus size, round multipliers) from a primary or reliable secondary source.
- I could not verify how "Safety Quip" works. My unverified recollection is that Quiplash 2 and 3 offer a button that submits a pre-written fallback answer for reduced points. The Fandom wiki was inaccessible and search returned nothing, so treat this as unconfirmed.

## 3. Reveal sequence and pacing on the host screen

### Takeaway
The broad structure is confirmed:
- Two rounds of 1v1 prompts, then a final all-player round.
- Original game: "The Last Lash," where everyone answers one prompt and picks a top three.
- Quiplash 3: "Thriplash," three responses per prompt.
- The whole game runs about 15 minutes for 3 to 8 players.

I found no authoritative per-beat timings (in seconds).

### Cited Findings
- **Round structure.** Three rounds. Rounds 1 and 2 are the two-prompts-per-player 1v1 matchups. In the final round, all players answer one prompt and then pick the three funniest answers. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiplash))
- **Thriplash.** "Quiplash 3 has a new final round called Thriplash, where the prompts ask players to enter three responses." "That's three times the chances to be funny!" ([Jackbox: Quiplash 3](https://www.jackboxgames.com/quiplash-three)). One Reddit comment in a Jackbox AMA thread called Quiplash 3's round 3 a "massive improvement" over Quiplash 2's (search snippet, [r/NintendoSwitch AMA](https://nyc1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/NintendoSwitch/comments/jx8kjg/we_are_jackbox_games_makers_of_the_jackbox_party/gcvaksw)).
- **Official length and player count:** "3 - 8 Players," "15 Minutes." ([Jackbox: Quiplash 3](https://www.jackboxgames.com/quiplash-three))
- **Moderation before reveal.** Quiplash 3 "now has a moderation feature that allows responses to be seen prior to being displayed on screen," and "a new filtering option allows profanity and hateful speech to be blocked before even reaching moderation." ([Jackbox: Quiplash 3](https://www.jackboxgames.com/quiplash-three))
- **Comedy timing is level design (non-Jackbox).** Zoe Quinn's GDC comedy talk argues that comedy timing is effectively a level-design problem and that "you can only test a joke once," so teams must test early with fresh players. ([Game Developer, 2 Mar 2015](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/exploring-what-it-takes-to-make-a-funny-game))

### Inferences
- Observed order of beats per matchup. This is my recollection of gameplay, not a published spec; a designer should verify it by recording a session.
  1. The host screen shows the prompt and the host reads it aloud.
  2. Answer A appears, then answer B, each read aloud by the host.
  3. Players' phones switch to two vote buttons, under a short countdown.
  4. Votes are tallied visually. Voter avatars or icons fly to the winning side, and percentages appear.
  5. Author names are revealed under each answer.
  6. Points are awarded, with host commentary. A unanimous result triggers a "QUIPLASH!" sting; identical answers trigger a jinx.
  7. The game moves straight to the next matchup.
- The 15-minute runtime suggests matchups must be well under a minute each. With 8 players there are 8 matchups per round across two rounds, plus a finale. A designer rebuilding this should budget roughly 30 to 45 seconds per matchup and treat anything longer as pacing debt. This estimate is mine, not Jackbox's.
- The read-aloud host is the main tool against dead air. Every reveal beat is covered by voice or animation, so the room is never waiting in silence.
- Pre-reveal moderation is a pacing feature as well as a safety one. In a medical-humor game, a moderator queue lets a TA or event host catch HIPAA-adjacent or patient-identifying answers before they hit the shared screen.

### Gaps
- No primary or reliable secondary source gave answer-phase or vote-phase timer lengths, or animation durations. The Fandom pages likely hold these but returned HTTP 402.
- I found no Jackbox statement on how they handle dead air or inter-matchup pacing. The inferences above rest on gameplay observation and general principles.

## 4. The host/narrator: Schmitty, event-triggered narration, and energy

### Takeaway
Quiplash is hosted by an unseen voice character, Josh "Schmitty" Schmitstinstein. Schmitty is a returning character from Jellyvision's *You Don't Know Jack*, voiced by Phil Ridarelli, and he has hosted every Quiplash. The design lineage goes back to the YDKJ host philosophy: make the host seem "really there." Gottlieb later codified this philosophy in the "Jack Principles" / interactive conversation interface white paper.

### Cited Findings
- **Bilder on the casting:** "Phil Ritterelli [sic] is a character called Schmitty in the original You Don't Know Jack…and so he's the voice of Quiplash." He also says "there's a real comedy bent and a performative bent to a lot of the folks that work for us." ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs))
- **Credits.** IMDb credits Phil Ridarelli as the Quiplash 3 host, "Josh 'Schmitty' Schmittsteinstein." ([IMDb, Party Pack 7 cast](https://m.imdb.com/title/tt18553702/fullcredits/cast)). The spelling differs between sources: Nintendo Life has "Ritterelli," while IMDb and Fandom have "Ridarelli."
- **Fandom wiki (search snippet only):**
  - Schmitty is "a Jellyvision host turned Jackbox host voiced by Phil Ridarelli."
  - He has "hosted all games of Quiplash since the original standalone release."
  - He also hosts *You Don't Know Jack* and *Lie Swatter*.
  - He "has two voice-lines as opposed to the other hosts who have one" (the meaning of this is unclear from the snippet).
  - ([Jackbox Fandom: Schmitty](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Schmitty?so=search))
- **Host philosophy lineage:** "During the early development of JACK, Harry realized that the design principles used in the game to make the host sound like 'he was really there,' could be applied well beyond games." Gottlieb wrote a 1997 white paper on the "interactive conversation interface (iCi)." The *You Don't Know Jack* host was "a fictional, unseen character." (Search snippets: [Jellyvision, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jellyvision); [Medium: Please Read The Jack Principles](https://medium.com/the-charming-device/please-read-the-jack-principles-4a7ee4820875), body returned 403.)
- **Writing host lines in-house.** Breit "wrote and recorded the host lines" herself for *Dodo Re Mi* (Party Pack 10), which suggests directors and writers own host voice directly. ([Jackbox blog, 19 Apr 2024](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-pp10-directing))
- **Improv roots.** Breit credits Second City and Chicago improv ("The idea of 'yes and' is taking an idea and then building upon it"). ([WBUR/WFDD, 2025](https://www.wfdd.org/2025-10-23/how-chicagos-famous-second-city-comedy-club-fueled-blockbuster-party-game-jackbox))

### Inferences
- The host's job is to:
  - Read every prompt and answer aloud, so the room hears a professional comic delivery, not just text.
  - Fill transitions.
  - React to events (quiplash, jinx, close votes, blowouts, timeouts) with pre-recorded, situation-keyed lines, giving an illusion of awareness.
  - Act as the "straight man" who heightens the players' jokes rather than competing with them, consistent with Breit's "make your scene partner look good."
- For an original medical game, an unseen host with a defined persona (for example, a burned-out chief resident or an overly chipper hospital PA system) could be voiced with event-keyed line pools:
  - unanimous win
  - jinx
  - tie
  - nobody answered
  - comeback
  - round transition
  - final round
  - winner
- Line pools need enough variants that repeats are rare across a session.
- Reading answers aloud matters even more for remote play, where players may glance between Zoom and their phone.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the actual Jack Principles text (Medium returned 403), so specific principles such as "maintain pacing" or "create the illusion of awareness" are not quoted here.
- I found no Jackbox source describing Schmitty's line taxonomy, how many lines are recorded per event, or the exact triggers (quiplash sting, jinx line). The Fandom pages likely list these but were inaccessible.

## 5. Shared screen vs phone UX (including remote Zoom/Discord play)

### Takeaway
The shared screen is the stage: prompt, answers, host animation, votes, reveal. The phone is a private writing desk and ballot. This split is what makes anonymity, and therefore the reveal, possible. Remote play works by streaming the shared screen while each player keeps their own phone. Jackbox designed for this early: Quiplash was pitched partly because people loved playing Jackbox over streams.

### Cited Findings
- **Bilder on why phones:** "It's not just skinning a gamepad so you don't have to go out and buy another controller, it's because you need it." "You needed to be able to type or draw with your finger." ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs))
- **Private prompts, shared flow.** Prompts are presented "privately on their device," and the room or stream boundary "doesn't necessarily have to be the room it's played in," since games run on streams and video calls. ([Kula, 2023](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2023/04/30/critical-play-quiplash-judging-games-jonathan/))
- **Streaming as a design driver.** Jackbox took Quiplash to Kickstarter partly because people loved streaming the Party Pack games even with a slight delay. ([Cliqist, 2015](https://cliqist.com/2015/03/17/quiplash/), via search snippet). The Kickstarter sought US$15,000 and raised over US$30,000 from 1,600+ backers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiplash)).
- **Audience mode.** Bilder: "we added an audience mode which brought the ability for up to 10,000 people to join a single game session… intentionally to cater to the streamers and the Twitch community." ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs)). Quiplash was the first Jackbox game with an audience ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiplash)).
- **COVID and remote play.** Bilder and Brooke Hofer gave a GDC Summer talk on the highs and lows of gaining new players during the COVID lockdown, and Jackbox made *Quiplash 2 InterLASHional* in response to non-English players arriving during the pandemic. ([Thumbsticks](https://www.thumbsticks.com/how-lockdown-opened-up-jackbox-party-to-a-massive-new-audience/), search snippet; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiplash))

### Inferences
- Put on the shared screen everything that should be performed together: prompt, both answers, vote animation, author reveal, scores, host character.
- Keep on the phone only what must be private or input-driven: your assigned prompts, the text field, the timer, the vote buttons, and an optional "you won/lost" toast.
- Never show other players' answers on a phone before the shared reveal.
- In remote play, stream latency (Zoom or Discord screen share) means the shared screen lags the phones. Mitigations:
  - Use generous vote windows.
  - Show the vote buttons only after the answers are fully on the stream.
  - Show the answers on the phone ballot as well, so a lagging stream doesn't block voting.
- An all-phones mode, where there is no shared screen and each phone renders the reveal, loses the communal "everyone looks up at once" moment. A medical game targeting remote cohorts should either expect a screen-share host or synchronize the reveal animation across phones and rely on voice chat for the laugh.

### Gaps
- I found no Jackbox documentation of the exact phone UI states for Quiplash, or of how they handle stream lag. The GDC Summer COVID talk content was not retrievable beyond its description.

## 6. Audio, visual style, and animation choices

### Takeaway
Quiplash 1 and 2 used a minimalist, limited-animation cartoon look. Quiplash 3 moved to a claymation/stop-motion-style look. I found no Jackbox staff statement explaining why. Music for Quiplash 3 is by Andy Poland.

### Cited Findings
- **Art evolution.** "Previously, Quiplash had a minimalist art style with Limited Animation, which also carried over in Quiplash 2. In Quiplash 3, the art style has evolved into a stop-motion claymation style." (TV Tropes snippet, fan-edited: [TV Tropes: Jackbox Party Pack](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/TheJackboxPartyPack))
- **Reviewer descriptions:**
  - textured clay animation look ([Third Coast Review](https://thirdcoastreview.com/2020/10/29/review-jackbox-party-pack-7-may-be-the-best-party-pack-yet))
  - claymation aesthetic ([The SixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2020/10/16/the-jackbox-party-pack-7-review/))
  - a shift from a cartoon to a claymation-like style ([Gamepur](https://www.gamepur.com/reviews/review-the-jackbox-party-pack-7))
  - (all via search snippets)
- **Mixed reception.** One Steam commenter said it seems the same, but with some pretty clay visuals. ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1211630/discussions/0/2952628643855845863/))
- **Music.** The Quiplash 3 Original Soundtrack is by Andy Poland (search snippet from a podcast page: [ETAO podcast 90](https://etao.blog/2020/08/18/podcast-90/)).
- **Directors on fun.** Warren Arnold: "If I'm not having fun as a user, then the players at home likely aren't having fun either." Breit asks, "Does this make sense to the player?" and "Is this fun?" ([Jackbox blog, PP10 directing](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-pp10-directing))

### Inferences
- Across all three versions, the visual style stays simple and bold, so the players' text stays the star. The claymation update adds warmth and tactility without competing with the words on screen.
- A medical game should pick an art style with similar restraint, so the art frames the answers rather than upstaging them. Options include clay, paper cut-out, or vintage hospital-signage graphics.
- Stings (the unanimous "QUIPLASH!", jinx) act as audio exclamation points that tell the room "this moment matters." Their exact sound design is undocumented in the sources I found.

- I found no primary Jackbox source (blog, interview, GDC) on Quiplash 3's art direction rationale, audio direction, the stings, or crowd-reaction sounds. A Jackbox "Making The Jackbox Party Pack 3" page exists ([source](https://www.jackboxgames.com/making-the-jackbox-party-pack-3)) and may cover Quiplash 2; it was not fetched in this session.

## 7. Common failure modes in party-game comedy and Jackbox mitigations

### Takeaway
The documented failure modes are:
- Prompts that are too vague or overloaded.
- References that alienate part of the room.
- Inside jokes that exclude newcomers.
- Offensive answers on a shared screen.
- Discouraging loss feedback.
- Comedy playtests polluted by funny staff ("false positives").

Jackbox's documented mitigations are:
- Prompt rules (specific-but-not-overloaded, know your audience, answer it yourself).
- A broad-audience default.
- Moderation and filtering before reveal.
- A family-friendly bias in the base prompt set.
- Speed: "time to laughter."

### Cited Findings
- **Alienation:** "If you're alienating even 1-2 people, the game is a lot less fun." ([Ham, 2016](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt))
- **Over-specificity:** the "1940's erotic film for elephants at the St. Louis Zoo" counterexample. ([Ham, 2016](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt))
- **Unanswerable prompts:** "If you can't think of a good answer to your own prompt…" ([Ham, 2016](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt))
- **Inside-joke exclusion:** In a Tee K.O. session, a player who didn't recognize a reference "had no clue why we were laughing" and seemed uncomfortable. - **Harsh loss feedback:** the same critique calls Tee K.O.'s losing animation potentially "incredibly discouraging" and asks for "a gentler way to show shirts winning." ([Mechanics of Magic, 2025](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2025/04/11/critplay1-amaru/))

### Inferences
Medical-humor-specific failure modes and mitigations, extrapolated from the above:
- **Too insider.** Specialty jargon locks out other disciplines. Mitigate with audience tiers (see section 1) and a rule that every base prompt must be answerable by a first-year student in any discipline.
- **Too edgy.** Patient-mocking, death, or HIPAA-flavored humor can wound people, especially clinicians with real trauma. Mitigate with:
  - Prompt framing that punches at the system, the training, or the absurd (insurance, pagers, EHRs, cafeteria food, attendings), not at patients.
  - An optional moderator queue, as in Quiplash 3.
  - A profanity/hate filter.
  - A content-intensity setting.
- **One obvious answer.** A prompt like "The worst thing to hear from your surgeon" collapses to "Oops." The jinx mechanic helps, but writers should test each prompt by listing five different answers quickly, per Ham's "lots of quick ideas" rule.
- **Slow reveals.** Keep each matchup tight and voiced, and let the host or animation fill transitions.
- **Writers' false positives.** Playtest with fresh med, nursing, PA, and dental students, not the dev team, and measure time-to-first-laugh.
