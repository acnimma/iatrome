# Legal Boundaries: What an Original Medical-Humor Party Game Can Borrow from Quiplash / Drawful

> Informational research, not legal advice. Primary jurisdiction: United States; UK/EU notes are flagged where found. Research conducted November 2026. Several primary sources (USPTO TSDR, NHS inform terms page, the National Archives OGL page, Google Patents) blocked automated fetching, so some points rely on aggregators or secondary summaries and are marked as such. Items marked "(unverified in this session)" come from the researcher's background knowledge and should be checked before they are relied on.

## Copyright of game mechanics: idea/expression, § 102(b), FL-108, and the key cases

### Takeaway
US law consistently treats a game's rules, method of play and core mechanics (e.g., "write a funny answer to a prompt, then players vote head-to-head") as unprotectable ideas or systems. Copyright attaches to the *expression*: art, audiovisual presentation, characters, specific text, and in Tetris, a particular combination of visual design choices that were not dictated by the rules. The safe line is to copy the rules and invent all of the look, feel, text and characters yourself.

### Cited Findings
- **Statute:** 17 U.S.C. § 102(b) says copyright protection does not "extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work." — [17 U.S.C. § 102 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102)
- **Copyright Office FL-108 "Games"** (archived; marked as reviewed April 2016; the live URL now returns 404): copyright "does not protect the idea for a game, its name or title, or the method or methods for playing it". It says text describing the rules, or pictorial matter on the gameboard or container, may be registrable. — [FL-108, archived copy (UNT Web Archive)](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20170124184513/https://www.copyright.gov/fls/fl108.pdf); [FL-108 HTML, archived](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20081204201439/http://www.copyright.gov/fls/fl108.html)
- **Merger in games:** commentary notes that the Ninth Circuit often applies the merger doctrine to games because they consist of abstract rules and play ideas, while the visual design of a game, especially an abstract or fanciful one, is protected. — [Frankfurt Kurnit Klein & Selz, "When It Comes to Games…" (Games Law Summit Journal 2017)](https://fkks.com/uploads/news/Games-Law-Summit-Journal_Issue-1_2017.04.27_KANE.pdf); [Mondaq / FKKS, "practical limit… may only be skin deep"](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/gaming/591564/when-it-comes-to-games-the-practical-limit-to-united-states-copyright-protection-may-only-be-skin-deep)
- **Tetris Holding, LLC v. Xio Interactive, Inc.**, 863 F. Supp. 2d 394 (D.N.J. May 30, 2012; Wolfson, J.): summary judgment for Tetris on both copyright and trade dress. The court held that the *idea* of a game of rotating falling pieces into a field is unprotectable but the *design of the component parts* is protectable. It rejected Xio's argument that it copied only unprotectable rules, and held that merger and scènes à faire did not apply because there are "countless" ways to express a falling-block puzzle (e.g., Dr. Mario). Fair use was rejected because Xio copied a substantial amount. — [Loeb & Loeb summary (June 2012)](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2012/06/tetris-holding-llc-v-xio-interactive-inc); [Opinion PDF (Missouri S&T mirror)](https://web.mst.edu/canisr/computersandthelaw/cases/tetris.pdf); [JD Supra, "Videogame App Developer Breaks the Rules"](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/videogame-app-developer-breaks-the-rules-01318)
- **Tetris trade dress:** the court treated the colour and style of the pieces as "arbitrary flourishes" unrelated to function, i.e. non-functional trade dress. Xio did not dispute fame, secondary meaning or likelihood of confusion, and the court rejected copyright preemption of the trade dress claim. — [Loeb & Loeb summary](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2012/06/tetris-holding-llc-v-xio-interactive-inc)
- **Specific Tetris elements the court treated as protectable expression (unverified in this session; recalled from the opinion):** the 20x10 playfield dimensions, bright distinct colours per tetrimino, the "ghost" piece, the display of upcoming pieces, "garbage" lines, and pieces changing appearance as they lock. In total, near-identical visual copying of choices not dictated by the rules. Verify against the [opinion PDF](https://web.mst.edu/canisr/computersandthelaw/cases/tetris.pdf).
- **Spry Fox, LLC v. Lolapps, Inc. (6waves)** (W.D. Wash., filed Jan. 2012): on October 29, 2012 the court denied 6waves' motion to dismiss. It held that Spry Fox had plausibly alleged substantial similarity under the extrinsic and intrinsic tests between *Triple Town* and *Yeti Town* (a reskin swapping bears for yetis), while noting that Spry Fox's copyright gives it no monopoly over the underlying idea. The case settled: Yeti Town IP was transferred to Spry Fox, the terms are confidential, and the game was pulled. There is no merits ruling. — [Wikipedia: Spry Fox v. Lolapps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spry_Fox,_LLC_v._Lolapps,_Inc.); [Game Developer, "There is substantial similarity…"](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/there-is-substantial-similarity-between-i-triple-town-i-and-i-yeti-town-i---u-s-court); [MCV/Develop, settlement](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/triple-town-cloning-suit-settled/); [Pocket Gamer, settlement](https://www.pocketgamer.com/news/6waves-settles-with-spry-fox-in-yeti-town-triple-town-clone-debate/); [Patent Arcade analysis](https://www.patentarcade.com/?p=5131)
- **Why Spry Fox survived dismissal (commentary):** 6waves' strategy rested on "gameplay isn't copyrightable", and the court rejected that framing at the pleadings stage. The case is cited as showing that a close reskin copying the progression, structure and many expressive particulars can be actionable even when the theme is changed. — [Adweek / SocialTimes](https://www.adweek.com/?p=477652); [MCV](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/court-denies-motion-to-dismiss-spry-fox-vs-6waves)
- **DaVinci Editrice S.r.l. v. ZiKo Games, LLC** (S.D. Tex.; Rosenthal, J.): the 2014 ruling held that the shared rules and method of play of *Bang!* and *Legends of the Three Kingdoms* could not support infringement, though claims about roles/characters proceeded. State unfair-competition claims were preempted. The 2016 summary judgment, 183 F. Supp. 3d 820, went to the defendants: the games differed in art and theme, and the claimed character/role interactions were unprotectable mechanics. — [Justia, Doc 44 (2014)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/4:2013cv03415/1134359/44); [Justia, Doc 73 (2016)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/4:2013cv03415/1134359/73/); [GovInfo opinion PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-txsd-4_13-cv-03415/pdf/USCOURTS-txsd-4_13-cv-03415-0.pdf); [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/texas-court-affirms-game-mechanics-not-protected-under-copyright-law)
- **DaVinci reasoning per press:** the lack of a fixed progression of events in a competitive, randomized game weighed against treating gameplay as protectable expression. (This is press paraphrase; check the opinion.) — [TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/game-mechanics-arent-protected-copyright-according-texas-court-ruling); [MCV](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/game-mechanics-cannot-be-protected-by-copyright/)
- **Data East USA, Inc. v. Epyx, Inc.**, 862 F.2d 204 (9th Cir. 1988): reversed an infringement finding and injunction (*Karate Champ* v. *World Karate Championship*). Similarities flowing from the sport of karate itself and from hardware constraints were unprotectable (scènes à faire). The district court erred by not limiting protection to Data East's original contributions, such as specific scoreboard and background design. — [Wikipedia: Data East v. Epyx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_East_USA,_Inc._v._Epyx,_Inc.); [Leagle, opinion text](https://www.leagle.com/decision/19881066862f2d20411030); [Patent Arcade case note](https://www.patentarcade.com/?p=1186)
- **Clone-wars context:** a broader survey of video-game cloning litigation sets these cases side by side (Data East, Tetris, Spry Fox, etc.). — [Medium, "Clones Wars: Video Game Litigation Illustrated"](https://medium.com/patents-technology-law/clones-wars-video-game-litigation-illustrated-36682abb4d68)

### Inferences
- Freely reusable (US): the loop of prompt, then private written answers, then two answers shown anonymously side by side, then audience vote, then points and a "sweep" bonus. Also rounds of escalating value, a final round where everyone answers one prompt, audience participation, drawing-a-secret-prompt followed by fake-title bluffing (the Drawful/Fibbage loop), phone-as-controller with a shared host screen, and room codes. These are rules and methods of play under § 102(b) and FL-108, and DaVinci is the closest analogue: a party/social game with no fixed narrative progression.
- Highest-risk area: Tetris-style "look and feel" copying. Do not reproduce Jackbox's screen layouts, colour schemes, host-character presentation, animation timing or UI composition closely, even with a medical reskin. Spry Fox shows that a theme swap (bears to yetis) does not by itself defeat a substantial-similarity claim when the rest is copied closely.
- Medical theming helps: original art, an original host persona (e.g., a fictional attending physician), original sound, and medical-specific prompt content make the expressive layer clearly distinct, which is the factor that won DaVinci for the defendants.
- **UK/EU (unverified in this session):** the UK has no direct statutory equivalent of § 102(b), but UK and EU case law reaches a broadly similar result. *SAS Institute v World Programming* (CJEU C-406/10, 2012) held that functionality and programming languages are not protected expression. *Nova Productions v Mazooma Games* (EWCA 2007) held that a game's rules and general features were not protected as literary or artistic works. Verify before relying on these.

## Trademark: Quiplash, Jackbox, Drawful, Last Lash; similar names, "like Quiplash" marketing, logos and trade dress

### Takeaway
QUIPLASH, JACKBOX GAMES and FIBBAGE appear as registered US marks of Jackbox Games, Inc. (per aggregators, not yet verified on USPTO TSDR). Even unregistered names like DRAWFUL are protectable through common-law use and Lanham Act § 43(a). Any name with the "-lash" construction (e.g., "Quiplash Rx" or "Medlash") or a similar logo is risky. Truthful nominative reference ("a party game for fans of Jackbox-style games") is generally defensible if minimal and without implied endorsement, but it is safer to avoid it in names, logos, app-store keywords and ads.

### Cited Findings
- **QUIPLASH:** US serial 97884785, registration no. 7425654, owner Jackbox Games, Inc., filed April 12, 2023, registered June 25, 2024, standard-character word mark, attorney of record Aric S. Jacover. Aggregators disagree on classes: one lists Class 41 only, another Classes 9 and 41. — [Gleanmark](https://gleanmark.com/trademark/uspto-97884785); [Markinton](https://markinton.com/trademark/quiplash-97884785); [Justia (older snapshot showing "New Application" status, April 2023)](https://trademarks.justia.com/978/84/quiplash-97884785.html). Authoritative check: [USPTO TSDR 97884785](https://tsdr.uspto.gov/#caseNumber=97884785&caseType=SERIAL_NO&searchType=statusSearch)
- **Jackbox portfolio:** about 10 federal filings, with notable marks FIBBAGE, QUIPLASH, JACKBOX GAMES and YOU DON'T KNOW JACK. One aggregator reports 6 registered and 4 cancelled. — [TrademarkDir owner page](https://trademarkdir.com/owner/jackbox-games-inc); [Markinton company page](https://markinton.com/company/jackbox-games-inc); [Justia owner page](https://trademarks.justia.com/owners/jackbox-games-inc-2661242)
- **JACKBOX GAMES:** listed as serial 85959420. — [TrademarkDir](https://trademarkdir.com/trademark/jackbox-games-85959420)
- **FIBBAGE:** serial 86590012, reg. 4855109, live, registered November 2015. — [Markinton](https://markinton.com/trademark/fibbage-86590012)
- **DRAWFUL and LAST LASH:** no federal filing by Jackbox found in the aggregator listings searched. Many unrelated "DRAW-" marks exist (e.g., DRAW SOMETHING). — [Gleanmark owner listing](https://gleanmark.com/owner/jackboxgamesinc); [Justia, DRAW SOMETHING](https://trademark.justia.com/869/77/draw-86977161.html)
- **Short phrases and titles get trademark rather than copyright protection:** the Copyright Office notes that short phrases "may also be eligible for trademark protection if used to identify goods or services". — [FKKS Advertising Law, "Can you copyright ad slogans and short phrases"](https://advertisinglaw.fkks.com/post/102i87d/can-you-copyright-ad-slogans-and-short-phrases)
- **Trade dress precedent in games:** in Tetris v. Xio, the non-functional visual style of the pieces was protected as trade dress, and confusion was undisputed. — [Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2012/06/tetris-holding-llc-v-xio-interactive-inc)
- **Spry Fox** also pleaded Lanham Act trademark claims alongside copyright. — [Wikipedia: Spry Fox v. Lolapps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spry_Fox,_LLC_v._Lolapps,_Inc.)
- **Nominative fair use (9th Cir., New Kids on the Block test):** (1) the product is not readily identifiable without the mark; (2) only so much of the mark is used as is reasonably necessary; (3) nothing suggests sponsorship or endorsement. In the Ninth Circuit this replaces the Sleekcraft confusion test when the defense is raised. — [Wikipedia: Nominative use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominative_use); [Playboy Enterprises v. Welles, 279 F.3d 796 (9th Cir. 2002) (Justia)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/279/796/506072)
- **Circuit split:** the Third Circuit uses a two-step approach, the Second Circuit treats the New Kids factors as additional to its Polaroid factors, and the Fifth Circuit has its own variant. — [Arnold & Porter (Nov. 2016), "Second Circuit Expands Split"](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2016/11/2016_11_16_second_circuit_expands_split__13324); [CCBJ article on nominative fair use](https://ccbjournal.com/articles/beach-boy-playmate-and-five-guys-boston-walk-courtroom-nominative-fair-use-trademarks)
- **Commercial uses can still be nominative:** Playboy v. Welles held that a use can be nominative even when it is commercial. — [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/279/796/506072)

### Inferences
- **Name:** pick a name with no "-lash", "quip-", "-ful", "Jack-" or "box" structure echoing Jackbox marks. A medical portmanteau unrelated to Quiplash's construction is low-risk. Run a USPTO search (Class 9 and 41), an EUIPO/UKIPO search if marketing in the UK/EU, and an app-store/Steam search before committing.
- **Marketing copy:** "Inspired by party games like Quiplash" in a blog post or FAQ is likely nominative (factor 1 is met because you are referring to that specific game). Risk rises if the mark appears in the title, logo, domain, SEO keywords or ad bids, if it uses Jackbox's stylised logo, or if the wording suggests affiliation. A visible disclaimer ("not affiliated with Jackbox Games") supports factor 3. Free-to-play status does not remove Lanham Act exposure, which turns on "use in commerce" and confusion, not on profit.
- **Trade dress:** avoid Jackbox's distinctive visual identity: the Quiplash colour palette and typographic style, the "VS" vote screen composition, the jackbox.tv-style join page, and the four-letter room-code-on-coloured-banner look. Mechanics such as room codes are free, but the specific presentation could be argued as trade dress if it is distinctive and non-functional (Tetris "arbitrary flourishes" logic).
- **UK/EU:** comparative advertising is governed by the Business Protection from Misleading Marketing Regulations 2008 (UK) and Directive 2006/114/EC (EU), which allow comparisons only under strict conditions (unverified in this session). Casual "like Quiplash" phrasing may not qualify as compliant comparative advertising, so keep it out of UK/EU-facing marketing.

## Prompt text: copyrightability of short prompts, prompt collections as compilations, and the fill-in-the-blank / head-to-head vote formats

### Takeaway
Individual short prompts ("The worst thing to hear from your surgeon: ___") are likely uncopyrightable short phrases under 37 C.F.R. § 202.1(a). But Jackbox's full prompt library is almost certainly protected as a compilation (creative selection and arrangement) and many longer prompts may be individually original. Bulk copying, or systematic paraphrase of their list, is risky. The formats themselves (fill-in-the-blank, anonymous head-to-head vote) are free ideas/methods.

### Cited Findings
- 37 C.F.R. § 202.1(a) excludes "words and short phrases such as names, titles, and slogans" from copyright. The Second Circuit called it a fair summary of the law in *Kitchens of Sara Lee v. Nifty Foods*, 266 F.2d 541, 544 (2d Cir. 1959). — [Columbia Journal of Law & the Arts article](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/lawandarts/article/download/5877/2888/10210); [Bitlaw, 37 CFR 202.1](https://www.bitlaw.com/source/37cfr/202_1.html)
- The Copyright Office refuses short phrases "even if the short phrase is novel or distinctive or lends itself to a play on words", treating them as de minimis authorship. — [Copyright Office Review Board decision ("Rub Dirt")](https://www.Copyright.gov/rulings-filings/review-board/docs/rub-dirt.pdf); [Review Board, "People Pleaser"](https://www.Copyright.gov/rulings-filings/review-board/docs/people-pleaser.pdf)
- Short phrases "may be protected as part of a larger, copyrighted work". The Office's originality analysis follows *Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991), under which unprotected elements can be protected when selected or arranged with sufficient creativity. — [FKKS Advertising Law](https://advertisinglaw.fkks.com/post/102i87d/can-you-copyright-ad-slogans-and-short-phrases); [Review Board, "Longer Lasting"](https://www.copyright.gov/rulings-filings/review-board/docs/longer-lasting.pdf)
- Under FL-108, "text matter describing the rules" is registrable while the method of play is not, so the expression of instructions is protected but the format is not. — [FL-108 archived](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20170124184513/https://www.copyright.gov/fls/fl108.pdf)
- In *DaVinci*, rules and the method of play shared between two card games did not support infringement. — [Justia, Doc 44](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/4:2013cv03415/1134359/44)
- Compilation protection (17 U.S.C. § 103) extends only to the compiler's original selection, coordination and arrangement, not to unprotectable components, per *Feist* and § 103(b). — [17 U.S.C. § 103 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/103)

### Inferences
- **Safe:** writing every prompt from scratch, using the fill-in-the-blank and head-to-head vote formats, and using common joke frames ("Worst thing to say during ___", "A rejected name for ___") that are generic and scènes à faire for comedy writing.
- **Risky:** scraping or transcribing Quiplash prompt lists (fan wikis host many), copying even a modest subset verbatim, or paraphrasing their list one-to-one. Even if each line is a short phrase, the selection is the protected compilation, and longer, more creative prompts may be individually protected. Many prompts are also not that short.
- **Process control:** keep an authorship log for the prompt bank, with writer, date and source (originally written, or derived from public-domain or OGL facts). This is good evidence of independent creation if challenged.
- **AI-generated prompts:** the US Copyright Office's position is that purely AI-generated text lacks human authorship (not researched in this session). Prompts generated by an LLM may be unprotectable by you. They also risk regurgitating memorised Quiplash prompts, so screen them against known Jackbox prompts.
- **UK/EU:** EU originality ("author's own intellectual creation", *Infopaq*, C-5/08) can in principle protect extracts as short as 11 words (unverified in this session). That is somewhat more protective of short text than the US short-phrase rule, so verbatim copying of even short prompts is riskier in the UK/EU. The EU/UK sui generis database right may also protect a prompt database where there was substantial investment (unverified in this session).

### Gaps
- No case law was found specifically on copyright in party-game prompt cards (e.g., Cards Against Humanity disputes) within this session's budget.
- The Copyright Office's AI-authorship guidance and the EU/UK points were not verified here.

## Medical content: condition names, NHS inform / Crown copyright / OGL v3.0, real people and institutions

### Takeaway
Medical facts and condition names (e.g., "appendicitis") are not copyrightable. They are facts or short phrases, and copyright cannot protect them (*Feist*, § 102(b), § 202.1). Copying NHS inform's prose text is a different matter. NHS public-sector content is generally reusable under the Open Government Licence v3.0 with the attribution line "Contains public sector information licensed under the Open Government Licence v3.0". The OGL excludes logos, third-party material and personal data, and forbids implying endorsement. Real historical physicians are generally safe for parody when long dead, but avoid living people, real hospitals and patient-like identifiable details.

### Cited Findings
- **Facts and short phrases are uncopyrightable:** the *Feist* originality requirement and the § 202.1 exclusion for names and short phrases (see above). — [17 U.S.C. § 102 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102); [Bitlaw, 37 CFR 202.1](https://www.bitlaw.com/source/37cfr/202_1.html)
- **nhs.uk terms:** NHS website content is released free of charge under the current OGL, subject to exceptions on the site. Reusers must include the attribution "Contains public sector information licensed under the Open Government Licence v3.0." — [NHS.uk terms and conditions](https://www.nhs.uk/our-policies/terms-and-conditions/)
- NHS.uk content is usually under Crown copyright and the OGL, but individual NHS Trusts may require specific permission. — [UCL Library, copyright for NHS users](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/library/learning-teaching-support/ucl-copyright-advice/copyright-nhs-users); [HEE Library support article](https://support.library.hee.nhs.uk/support/solutions/articles/27000124231)
- **OGL as default:** the OGL is the default licence for Crown bodies and recommended for all public sector bodies. — [National Archives, Open Government Licence](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/information-management/re-using-public-sector-information/uk-government-licensing-framework/open-government-licence/)
- **Third-party material:** Crown copyright licensing does not extend to material owned by others; "the onus for obtaining the consent of the copyright owner rests with the person or organisation who wishes to re-use it". — [National Archives, Crown copyright and our website](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/legal/copyright/crown-copyright-and-our-website/)
- **OGL v3.0 exemptions** (from mirrors of the licence text): personal data; departmental or public sector logos, crests and the Royal Arms (except where integral to a document or dataset); military insignia; third-party rights the provider cannot license; other IP such as patents, trade marks and design rights; and identity documents. — [SPDX, OGL-UK-3.0 text](https://spdx.org/licenses/OGL-UK-3.0.html); [Wikipedia, Open Government Licence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Government_Licence)
- **OGL non-endorsement:** the licence grants no right to use the information in a way that suggests any official status or endorsement. — [National Archives FOI response CAS-282114](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/freedom-of-information/requests/cas-282114/); [SPDX, OGL-UK-3.0](https://spdx.org/licenses/OGL-UK-3.0.html)
- **OGL permits commercial and non-commercial reuse and adaptation**, subject to attribution. (The general licence grant; see the licence text.) — [SPDX, OGL-UK-3.0](https://spdx.org/licenses/OGL-UK-3.0.html)
- **Right of publicity (US):** this is state law with no federal right. About 24 states (including CA, NY, FL, TX) recognise a post-mortem right, for durations such as 70 years in California and 40 in New York. New York's statute covers "personalities" whose likeness had commercial value at death and exempts parody, satire, commentary, criticism and historical works. The First Amendment strongly protects expressive works. — [Fish & Richardson, NY post-mortem right of publicity](https://www.fr.com/news-insights/articles/new-york-passes-post-mortem-right-of-publicity-law/); [International Documentary Association, post-mortem publicity](https://www.documentary.org/column/raising-dead-understanding-post-mortem-rights-publicity)

### Inferences
- **NHS inform specifically:** NHS inform is run by NHS 24 for Scotland. I could not retrieve its own terms (nhsinform.scot returned 403). It is likely, but unconfirmed, that it is Crown copyright under the OGL, as nhs.uk is. Its A–Z list of condition names is facts plus a compilation. Using individual condition names, or building your own list from general medical knowledge, does not need a licence. Copying its descriptive paragraphs, structure or images does; attribute under the OGL if its terms allow it, and check for third-party images and logos, which are excluded.
- **Practical rule:** write all medical flavour text originally. Use the OGL attribution only if you actually reuse NHS prose. Never use NHS logos or the blue NHS lozenge; the NHS identity is a protected trademark and is excluded from the OGL. Avoid anything that implies NHS endorsement, which the non-endorsement clause forbids and which matters for a health-adjacent product.
- **Historical physicians:** jokes about long-dead figures (Hippocrates, Galen, Lister, Semmelweis) are low-risk. Most died far outside any post-mortem window, and parody or historical exceptions apply. Living or recently deceased doctors, including celebrity TV doctors, carry defamation and publicity risk; use fictional characters instead. A UK note: the UK has no general right of publicity, but passing off (false endorsement, e.g. *Irvine v Talksport*) and defamation apply (unverified in this session).
- **Real institutions:** avoid naming real hospitals, trusts, pharma companies or drug brand names in jokes. Brand drug names are trademarks, and disparaging jokes about real institutions invite trademark/dilution or defamation complaints. Generic drug names (INNs) and condition names are free to use.
- **Health misinformation:** a humour game should carry a "not medical advice" disclaimer and avoid jokes that could be read as genuine treatment guidance (product-safety and reputational reasoning, not a specific legal rule found).

### Gaps

- The OGL text was read via mirrors (SPDX), not the National Archives original, which returned 403.
- No source was found on defamation risk for jokes about deceased persons. Generally, in both the US and the UK, the dead cannot be defamed (unverified in this session).

## Fonts: "personal use only" / demo fonts in a free public web game, commercial use, and @font-face embedding

### Takeaway
Do not use "personal use only" or demo fonts in a publicly deployed web game, even a free one. Serving a font via @font-face distributes the font file to every visitor, and desktop or personal licences generally do not cover web embedding. Many demo licences also treat ads, donations or sponsorship as commercial. Use fonts under the SIL Open Font License (e.g., Google Fonts), which allows web embedding, or buy a proper webfont licence.

### Cited Findings
- Typical demo-font terms say "DEMO VERSION… ONLY allowed for PERSONAL USE" and "NO COMMERCIAL USE ALLOWED", and direct buyers to a paid commercial licence. Some demo listings ambiguously say it "can be used on your website". — [cdnfonts, Caliyan Demo](https://www.cdnfonts.com/caliyandemo.font); [cdnfonts, Scripty Demo](https://www.cdnfonts.com/scripty-demo.font)
- Some free licences explicitly allow web embedding. One Font Squirrel-hosted licence says the font is for personal and commercial use and that using it "for a @font-face declaration is permitted". This shows web embedding is a right that must be granted. — [Font Squirrel licence example](https://www.fontsquirrel.com/fonts/Daela?q=Amatic)
- Font Squirrel's webfont generator requires users to confirm that fonts are legally eligible for web embedding. — [Fontspring / Font Squirrel @font-face guidance](https://www.fontspring.com/fontface)
- Desktop and web licences are separate. A desktop licence covers installation on computers for design work; a webfont licence covers serving and displaying the font on a website. — [TypeTogether, "Understanding webfont licensing"](https://www.type-together.com/understanding-webfont-licensing); [Dalton Maag licensing](https://www.daltonmaag.com/licensing)
- Webfont licences commonly allow self-hosting with prepaid pageview tiers or per-domain fees. "There is no definitive standard", and terms vary by foundry; some require the foundry's own hosting service. — [TypeTogether](https://www.type-together.com/understanding-webfont-licensing); [Happy Cog, "Webfont licensing 101"](https://www.happycog.com/insights/webfont-licensing-101); [Colophon Foundry licence FAQ](https://direct.colophon-foundry.org/information/licence-faq)
- A forum discussion notes that a designer may view public web hosting as no longer "personal", even without revenue. This is a forum opinion, not authority. — [DaFont forum thread](https://www.dafont.com/forum/read/186840/css-script-for-fonts)

### Inferences
- **"Personal use" usually means private, non-public use** (your own invitations, school projects). A public website game played by strangers is publication and distribution of the font file, so it falls outside most personal-use grants regardless of revenue. Each EULA defines this differently; read the actual licence.
- **"Commercial" triggers:** in most demo EULAs, ads, donations or tip jars (Ko-fi/Patreon), sponsorships, paid upgrades, cosmetics, or use to promote a business would count as commercial. Several foundries treat any use by a company, or any use that promotes a brand, as commercial. A free game with a donation button is very likely commercial for licensing purposes.
- **Safe path (unverified in this session, but standard):** SIL Open Font License 1.1 fonts (most of Google Fonts) may be used, embedded, self-hosted and bundled, commercially or not. You cannot sell the font by itself, and modified versions cannot use reserved font names. Fonts under Apache 2.0 or Ubuntu Font Licence are similarly permissive. Keep the licence file alongside self-hosted font files.
- **Typeface designs vs font files:** in the US, typeface designs are not copyrightable, but font software is protected as a computer program (a well-known position, not re-verified here). The risk lies in using the file, not in the letterforms. In the UK, typefaces can attract copyright and design rights (unverified in this session).
- **Licensing a demo font:** if a specific display font is essential, buy its webfont licence (not just desktop) and confirm it covers self-hosting via @font-face, the expected pageviews, and use in a game UI. Some foundries treat apps or games as a separate "app/embedding" licence.

- No authoritative cross-industry definition of "commercial use" for fonts exists, and this session found none. It is licence-specific.
- The US position on typeface copyright (37 C.F.R. § 202.1(e)) and the UK typeface position were not verified here.
