# Two Anonymous Answers Win the Room

Quiplash works because the basic unit of play, two anonymous answers to one prompt followed by a vote from everyone else, gets a room laughing almost immediately. It needs nearly no explanation, it creates a built-in "versus" punchline, and it shields people from the social risk of telling a joke until the room has already reacted. Everything else serves that duel. Scores come from **share of the vote, not raw vote counts**, so a 3-player living-room game and a stream with 10,000 viewers score on the same scale. Round values escalate (Round 2 doubles). A unanimous "Quiplash" bonus rewards the answer the whole room agrees on, and a "Jinx" zeroes out identical answers. An unseen host reads every prompt and answer aloud so there is never dead air. All of this can be adapted faithfully: in US law, rules and methods of play are unprotectable ideas, and copyright and trademark attach only to Jackbox's names, art, host character, prompt text, screen layouts and fonts. A medical-humor version therefore needs to rebuild the *loop* and invent every visible and audible layer. Three risks matter most for this audience. The first is **specialty jargon that locks out mixed med/nursing/PA/dental rooms**. The second is **patient-directed or identifying humor on a shared screen**. The third is **short room codes that let griefers find open lobbies by brute force**. A further caveat: Jackbox has never published its scoring formula or timer lengths. Every exact number below comes from fan wikis that sometimes disagree with each other, and the report flags each disagreement.

## Time to laughter is zero because every prompt is a duel

Jackbox CEO Mike Bilder judges every prototype by one question, "how fast do you get to laughter?" His verdict on Quiplash: **"Time to laughter is like zero… Every time we played it, it was funny,"** and it "needed almost no explanation on how to play" ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs)). Quiplash started as a stripped-down *Fibbage*. The team took a game where players write fake trivia answers and others vote, then removed the trivia, so the comedy was all that was left (same source). That origin explains why so much of Quiplash's appeal is structural. The game has no rules about what is correct, and the official page tells players there are "no rules or correct answers" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiplash)). Players bring all the content. The game supplies a diving board, a comparison, and a stage.

The head-to-head format generates three laughs per matchup. First, the prompt sets up an expectation. Second, two punchlines land, and the contrast between them is a joke of its own. Third, the author reveal recontextualizes everything, as when the quietest person in the room turns out to have written the filthiest answer. A Stanford critical-play analysis says the separate phone screens give players "a moment of safety where their identity isn't known," letting the room "get used to the answers before players' names are revealed." It adds that impersonal judging "prevents people ganging up on winners" ([Mechanics of Magic, Kula 2023](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2023/04/30/critical-play-quiplash-judging-games-jonathan/)). Jackbox creative director Brooke Breit, trained in improv at Second City, frames prompts in improv terms: "making your scene partner look good" and "giving you the diving board so that you can jump in" ([WFDD/WBUR, 2025](https://www.wfdd.org/2025-10-23/how-chicagos-famous-second-city-comedy-club-fueled-blockbuster-party-game-jackbox)). In this design, competition is a way to push players to be funnier, and points matter less than the laughs.

For a clinical audience, anonymity does more work than it does at a typical party. Clinical training is steeply hierarchical. A nursing student who anonymously beats an attending, and finds out only at the reveal, gets a social payoff the game creates by design. The adaptation should therefore protect anonymity strictly. Authors stay hidden until votes lock, and the author reveal gets its own beat. Organizers of faculty-mixed events should be able to hide author names entirely.

The split between a phone and a shared screen is what makes this anonymity possible. Bilder insists the phone is "not just skinning a gamepad… it's because you need it… to type" ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs)). The shared screen is where the group watches the performance together, and the phone is a private desk and ballot. The join flow has no app: players visit a URL in the phone browser and enter a four-letter code shown on the host screen ([Jackbox, How to Play](https://www.jackboxgames.com/how-to-play)). This zero-install onboarding is what a browser-based medical game most needs to copy, and copying it is legally free.

## Three rounds, two prompts each, and a final that changed every version

Every Quiplash has three rounds for **3–8 players** plus an optional audience, and an official runtime of **about 15 minutes** ([Jackbox: Quiplash 3](https://www.jackboxgames.com/games/quiplash-3)). In Rounds 1 and 2, each player answers **two prompts** within one shared "allotted time," and each prompt goes to exactly **two players** ([Fandom: Quiplash series](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series)); [Jackboxpedia: Quiplash](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash)). That arithmetic solves odd player counts in the main rounds with no special handling. N players × 2 prompts ÷ 2 answers per prompt = **N matchups per round**. A ring assignment works for any N ≥ 3: prompt *i* goes to players *i* and *i+1*, so each player faces two different opponents. Jackbox has not published its actual assignment algorithm, so the ring is the minimal structure that satisfies the documented rules rather than a confirmed copy of it. Each matchup is voted on by the N−2 players who are not in it, plus the audience. A 3-player game therefore has a single voter per matchup, which is why the sweep achievements require **5+ players** ([Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series))). An 8-player game produces 16 head-to-head reveals before the final.

The final round is where the versions differ most.

| Version (year) | Final round | How it works | Voting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quiplash 1 (2015) / XL (2015) | The Last Lash | Every player answers one shared prompt | Each player gets **3 votes, stackable** on one answer; audience gets 1 (Jackboxpedia says 3). A **3,000-point pool** is split at 30 points per 1% of votes |
| Quiplash 2 (2016) / InterLASHional (2020) | The Last Lash | One shared prompt in one of three formats: **Acro-Lash** (make an acronym from three letters), **Word-Lash** (use a given word), **Comic-Lash** (fill a blank speech bubble) | **Gold/silver/bronze medals to three different answers** (gold/silver only with 3–4 players); audience gets 1 vote |
| Quiplash 3 (2020) | Thriplash | Back to head-to-head pairs, but each player writes **three answers** to one prompt | Voters pick the better *set* of three; **no safety quip allowed**; reported maximum of 6,000 points per prompt |

Sources: [Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series)); [Jackboxpedia Q1](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash); [Jackboxpedia Q2](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_2); [Jackboxpedia Q3](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_3); [Jackbox: Quiplash 3](https://www.jackboxgames.com/games/quiplash-3).

Each change fixes a specific problem. Quiplash 1 lets players stack votes, so one answer can take an overwhelming share. Quiplash 2's medals force voters to rank answers, which spreads points more evenly. Its custom-prompt episodes cannot replace the Last Lash, which "will always be one of the three above-mentioned prompt types" ([Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series))). Thriplash ended the all-versus-all final altogether and brought back the duel. That reintroduced the odd-count problem, because pairs make N/2 matchups. Jackbox solved it with the host: **Schmitty plays against whoever is in last place after Round 2**, using a pre-written answer that always appears on the left ([Jackboxpedia Q3](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_3)). Thriplash answers are revealed in order, which invites a rule-of-three build with the third line as the punchline. Critics split on it: Geek Outpost called it "the best iteration of the game yet" ([Geek Outpost](https://geekoutpost.com/jackbox-party-pack-7-review/)). Steam reviewers of the older version list a "disappointing Final Lash" among their recurring complaints ([Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/1111940/quiplash_2_inter_las_hional/stats/details)).

For the medical adaptation, the Thriplash model is the better base. It keeps the duel's tension and anonymity into the finale, it gives a natural slot for a host-persona opponent, and three answers per prompt produce a rich, list-shaped joke format. Examples include "three things you should never say during a sterile procedure" or "three rejected hospital PA announcements." An optional all-play "group final" in the Quiplash 2 style can rotate in for variety. Acronyms are a natural fit for medicine: a three-letter "Acro" round mocking clinical abbreviation culture would land with every discipline.

## Scoring rewards vote share, punishes the obvious, and forgives the stuck

The best-documented formula, from the Fandom wiki and applicable to the series generally, is **10 points per 1% of the vote plus a 100-point winner bonus in Round 1, all doubled in Round 2**. A **"Quiplash"** happens when an answer gets every player's vote and more than half of the audience's, and it earns **250 points instead of the 100 winner bonus**. If more than 90% of the audience also agrees, it becomes a "Super Quiplash." Identical answers are declared a **JINX** and nobody scores. If a player misses the timer or has their answer censored, **the opponent receives all the points** ([Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series))). Jackboxpedia describes Quiplash 1 as a pool with a winner bonus "worth 10% of that pool" and a sweep bonus of "25%," and splits points evenly on a tie. That matches Fandom exactly if the pool is 1,000 per prompt ([Jackboxpedia Q1](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash)). The sources disagree on two points. Jackboxpedia's Quiplash 2 page gives the sweep bonus as 100/200 by round, which may actually describe the ordinary winner bonus ([Jackboxpedia Q2](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_2)). Its Quiplash 3 page gives a maximum of 1,000 / 2,000 / 6,000 per prompt across the three rounds and calls the last "tripled," which is internally inconsistent: 6,000 is triple the Round 2 maximum but six times Round 1 ([Jackboxpedia Q3](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_3)).

| Round 1 example (10 voters) | Vote share points | Bonus | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7–3 winner | 700 | +100 winner | **800** |
| 7–3 loser | 300 | — | **300** |
| 5–5 tie | 500 each | none | **500 each** |
| 10–0 sweep ("Quiplash") | 1,000 | +250 | **1,250** |
| Round 2 | all values ×2 | ×2 | e.g. sweep = 2,500 |

Each rule has a design purpose. Percentage scoring makes scores independent of crowd size. The jinx punishes the first idea everyone has. The sweep bonus rewards the answer the whole room recognizes as the best. Together they push players away from the obvious answer and toward ones that are surprising but true. The escalating multiplier keeps last-place players in contention, and the Quiplash 3 achievement "Worst to First" shows that comebacks are an intended part of play ([Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series))). Quiplash 3 even has a dedicated graphic for a matchup in which nobody votes.

**Safety quips**, introduced in Quiplash 2, let a stuck player tap for a canned answer that **earns half points**. Quiplash 3 reworked them to be "decently related to the prompt," and they are disabled in Thriplash ([Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series)); [Jackboxpedia Q3](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_3)). They can still win, and can even sweep: there are achievements for winning, and for getting a Quiplash, with one. No source documents a per-game limit. Timing out does *not* auto-submit a safety quip; it forfeits the matchup. For tired clinicians playing after a shift, a safety quip is a kindness that keeps the game moving without rewarding passivity.

**Timers are undocumented.** No official or wiki source gives the length of the answer or vote phase. Here is what is known. Party Pack 2 introduced an "extend the timer" option. Jackbox recommends Extended Timers for streams "to compensate for possible stream delays," and they can be changed mid-game ([Jackbox PP8 features](https://www.jackboxgames.com/streaming-moderation-accessibility-features-jackbox-party-pack-eight/)). The writing phase moves on as soon as everyone has submitted. The original game's vote timer "doesn't automatically zero out once all the votes are cast," which a reviewer counted as a flaw ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/90364/quiplash-review-just-add-people-and-lash-away)). The adaptation should end every phase early once all inputs are in. Its starting values have to come from playtesting. A reasonable proposal, not a Jackbox figure, is about 90 seconds to write two prompts, about 15 seconds to vote, and a 1.5× extended option for Zoom play. The 15-minute runtime across 16+ matchups implies roughly **30–45 seconds per matchup** for reveal, vote and score, and anything longer adds up into a slow game.

### Audience votes need a published rule Jackbox never wrote

Quiplash introduced Jackbox's audience mode. The original launched in 2015 supporting **10,000 participants** through the same URL and code, up from earlier caps of 100 ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2015-06-30-quiplash-10000-players-twitch.html)), and Bilder says it was built "intentionally to cater to the streamers and the Twitch community" ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs)). Party Pack 2 capped XL's audience at 200 ([Fandom PP2](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/The_Jackbox_Party_Pack_2)), and Party Pack 3 raised it to 10,000 ([Fandom PP3](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/The_Jackbox_Party_Pack_3)). The audience votes in every version. Only Quiplash 2 and InterLASHional let the audience write, via a collective "Audience Play-Along" answer to one prompt per round ([Jackbox audience matrix](https://www.jackboxgames.com/how-audience-play-along-differs-in-each-jackbox-game)). Quiplash 3 dropped audience writing, plausibly because of the moderation burden.

Jackbox publishes no rule for weighting audience votes against player votes. The only documented rule treats the two as separate groups for the sweep test ("all the players and more than half of the audience"), and a claim that audience votes count for fewer points remains unverified ([Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series))). A pure percentage over a large audience would let viewers decide every match. The adaptation should publish an explicit rule. One example is to count the audience as a single block worth a fixed share (say one-third) of each matchup's vote, with player votes making up the rest. For a lecture-hall setting, where a class of 150 votes on 8 contestants, that cap is what keeps contestants' choices meaningful.

## The host and the prompt carry the comedy

Quiplash's host is **Josh "Schmitty" Schmitstinstein**, an unseen voice played by Phil Ridarelli and carried over from *You Don't Know Jack* ([IMDb](https://m.imdb.com/title/tt18553702/fullcredits/cast); [Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/time-to-laughter-is-like-zero-jackbox-games-ceo-on-quiplash-t-shirts-and-10-years-of-party-packs)). The host's job is to read every prompt and answer with professional comic delivery, cover every transition so the room never waits in silence, and react to events (quiplash, jinx, ties, blowouts, timeouts) with pre-recorded lines matched to each situation, which makes the game seem aware of what is happening. In Quiplash 3, Schmitty also substitutes as the odd-count opponent, appearing as an unused avatar "with an added beard and moustache" ([Fandom: Schmitty](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Schmitty)). At Jackbox, directors write host lines themselves: Breit "wrote and recorded the host lines" for *Dodo Re Mi* ([Jackbox blog, PP10](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-pp10-directing)). No source documents Schmitty's line taxonomy or how many recordings exist per event.

The reveal sequence for each matchup is not published as a specification, and the version here is reconstructed from gameplay. The prompt is shown and read aloud. Answer A appears, then answer B, each read aloud. Phones switch to two vote buttons. Votes animate to the winning side with percentages. Authors are revealed. Points land, with a sting for a Quiplash or Jinx. The adaptation should keep that order, and especially never reveal authors before votes lock. It also needs an original host persona, voiced or text-to-speech, with event-keyed line pools deep enough that lines rarely repeat in a session. A burned-out chief resident or a relentlessly chipper overhead-page announcer would both fit. Reading aloud matters even more remotely, where a Zoom stream lags behind the phones. Show the answers on the phone ballot too, and open voting only once the stream has caught up.

Jackbox's prompt-writing guidance is short and directly reusable. Spencer Ham's rules are: you must be able to answer your own prompt; the prompt should "lend itself to a lot of quick ideas"; know your audience, because **"If you're alienating even 1-2 people, the game is a lot less fun"**; be specific but not overloaded; and vary the structure beyond fill-in-the-blank. His specificity example sets the calibration: "The title of an erotic movie" is too vague, "…for elephants" works, and "…a 1940's erotic film for elephants at the St. Louis Zoo" is overloaded ([Jackbox blog, Ham 2016](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt)). C.J. Tuor adds tactics that set the tone: funny words and absurd premises ("What the boogeyman is afraid of"), reliving past mistakes ("we transform our pain into art"), a broad audience by default, crude humor, and questions the writers sincerely want answered ([Jackbox blog, Tuor 2019](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/the-science-of-creating-a-quiplash-prompt)). Jackbox also prunes prompts that collapse to a single default answer; writer Tim Sniffen's note on Tee K.O. 2 drawings was "NO MORE PIZZA DRAWINGS" ([Jackbox blog, PP10](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-pp10-directing)).

These rules translate into a medical prompt standard. Applying Ham's calibration: "A rejected name for a medical device" is too vague, "A rejected name for a pediatric enema" works, and the overloaded version adds a decade and a state. The "past mistakes" tactic suits clinical training unusually well, because shared indignities cross disciplines: being pimped on rounds, the first code, charting at 3 a.m., the pager that never stops, board-exam dread, cafeteria food. Ham's alienation rule is the central risk. A nephrology-eponym prompt shuts out the dental students in a mixed room. Prompts should be tagged by audience breadth: universal clinical life, discipline-specific, and specialty-deep. Mixed rooms should default to the universal tier, and every base prompt should be answerable by a first-year student in any discipline. Medical card games show the opposite failure. *Quack in the Box* is "really funny for those in the medical field more than for outsiders" ([GeekDad](https://geekdad.com/2011/04/quack-in-the-box-makes-medical-malpractice-into-a-game/)), and a reviewer's non-medical husband "didn't get the jokes" in *What Do You Meme? Nurses Edition* ([Lattes & Literature](https://lattesandliterature.com/products/what-do-you-meme-career-series-nurses)). Prompts should also aim at the system rather than at patients: EHRs, insurance, pagers, attendings, scrubs, night shift. Each prompt should pass a five-answers-in-a-minute test, because "Worst thing to hear from your surgeon" collapses into "Oops" and will trigger jinxes. Finally, Jackbox's CEO warns that a staff of comedy writers produces "a lot of false positives" in playtests ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/is-the-game-funny-or-just-the-players-jackbox-ceo-says-they-get-a-lot-of-false-positives-with-a-staff-of-comedy-writers/)), and Zoe Quinn's GDC point that "you can only test a joke once" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/exploring-what-it-takes-to-make-a-funny-game)) leads to the same practice: playtest with fresh students, not the dev team, and track time-to-first-laugh as the main metric.

Visual style should stay restrained. Quiplash moved from minimalist limited animation to claymation in Quiplash 3 (reviewers' description, e.g. [Third Coast Review](https://thirdcoastreview.com/2020/10/29/review-jackbox-party-pack-7-may-be-the-best-party-pack-yet)), but in every version the players' text is the main attraction. A medical game could use paper cut-outs, clay, or vintage hospital-signage graphics, provided the look is clearly its own.

## Moderation arrived five years late and still leaks

Jackbox built its safety tools in layers, under pressure. Its first moderation tools were manual censoring by the host, a Twitch-login requirement, and room locking. In June 2020, Jackbox admitted that "too many streamers… have flat-out quit playing Jackbox Games" over hateful content and that it was still "far too easy to inject hateful messages." It announced that Quiplash 3 would let moderators reject content "*before* it is ever seen," plus an automatic word filter that "won't catch everything" ([Jackbox, Combating Hateful Speech](https://jackboxgames.com/combating-hateful-speech-and-harassment-in-jackbox-games)). The current toolset includes the following ([Jackbox blog, Safety and Moderation](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/safety-and-moderation-streaming-jackbox-games)):

- A Profanity Filter (None/Moderate/Strict).
- Moderation, where moderators approve or reject each submission at mod.jackbox.tv using a password that changes each time moderation is toggled ([Jackbox, Streaming guide](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/so-you-want-to-stream-jackbox)).
- A Family-Friendly filter that **filters only Jackbox's own content, never player text**.
- Passworded rooms, room-code hiding, start-from-controller, player limits, and Twitch login.

Kicking players arrived only with Party Pack 9 in 2022. A kick lasts only for that room, mid-game kicks are allowed only above the minimum player count, and only Twitch login stops a kicked user from rejoining ([Jackbox, Kick feature](https://www.jackboxgames.com/the-ability-to-kick-players-and-other-new-features-coming-to-party-pack-9/)). A Windows Central reviewer had warned in 2015 that the game "badly needs proper moderation features" ([Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/quiplash-review)).

Room codes are the weakest point. A public script, `jackboxjoiner.py`, polls `ecast.jackboxgames.com/room/<code>` across four-character codes. It joins any room whose response reports `joinAs: "player"` and `requiresPassword: false`, entering as "A GHOST," and skips passworded rooms ([GitHub gist](https://gist.github.com/zmarffy/259fcd0b096c55cdcc525bd54615908c)). Four letters give only 26⁴ = **456,976** codes, so a public lookup endpoint that reveals whether a room is joinable lets a griefer find live lobbies without ever seeing the stream. The fix is cheap and should be in the first release. Use six-character codes from a 32-symbol alphabet with no ambiguous characters (32⁶ ≈ **1.07 billion**). Expire codes when a game ends. Rate-limit lookups by IP and session, and never expose "requires password" or "joinable" status to anonymous callers. Lock player slots at game start, so late joiners become audience members. Offer an optional room passphrase for streamed or lecture sessions.

A medical game faces risks that Jackbox does not. Clinical humor is edgier than average, and some players carry real trauma from deaths, codes and burnout. A shared screen in a classroom can also show something that looks like protected health information, such as a real patient detail typed as a joke. The moderation design should therefore:

- Turn on server-side filtering of **answers and player names** by default.
- Give the host a one-tap "hide answer / hide name" control. A hidden answer forfeits the matchup, as it does in Jackbox ([Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series))).
- Provide an optional pre-reveal queue where a TA or event lead can approve answers.
- Add a content-intensity setting that also controls which prompt tiers are drawn.
- Allow crowd vote-kick for casual rooms, the norm in free games like skribbl.io, where "everyone can vote the person out" ([CrazyGames](https://www.crazygames.com/game/skribblio)).
- Show an onscreen reminder at join: no real patients, no identifiers.

Pre-reveal moderation also helps pacing, because an inappropriate answer is caught before it can stall the room.

## Stale prompts and missing groups are the real enemies

Players love Quiplash. Steam rates the original about **89.6% positive from roughly 1,979 reviews** ([SteamDB](https://steamdb.info/app/351510/charts)) and InterLASHional **92% of 336** ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1111940/)). The complaints, though, show where the formula wears out. One aggregator of InterLASHional reviews lists "Repetitive rounds and disappointing Final Lash," "lack of custom prompts," and crashes as the negative themes. Its playtime breakdown is the most useful benchmark: players with **0–2 hours were least positive (~80%)**, mid-range players (2–10 h) about 95% positive, and positive reviewers averaged about 11.5 hours ([Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/1111940/quiplash_2_inter_las_hional/stats/details)). First-session friction (technical trouble, too few players) costs more goodwill than long-term repetition does. The most-cited structural complaint is that the game needs a group, with no matchmaking. One reviewer explains staleness well: prompts wear out because "most people know what to say… if they got a banger response" (via [Vaporlens/SteamDB](https://steamdb.info/app/351510/charts)). By Quiplash 3, some critics found the formula tired. Screen Rant called it "easily the worst of the bunch" ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/jackbox-party-pack-7-game-review/)), and Bonus Stage called it "the most over-the-plate, don't rock the boat addition" ([Bonus Stage](https://www.bonusstage.co.uk/archives/177996)). Screen Rant added that without a custom-prompt mode a group "may as well just use pen and paper." Jackbox has not released a new mainline Quiplash since 2020: Party Pack 12 (October 2026) contains none ([Jackbox blog](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/here-are-all-5-games-in-the-jackbox-party-pack-12)).

**Prompt consumption explains the staleness.** An 8-player game uses about 8 + 8 prompts in Rounds 1–2 plus the final, roughly **17–20 prompts per game** (the final uses one shared prompt, or four Thriplash prompts). A pool of a few hundred prompts will start repeating within 10–20 sessions. Jackbox's only published counts are modest: XL added "over 100 brand-new prompts," and InterLASHional added 100 per language. User-made Quiplash 2 Episodes allowed **64 prompts per episode and 120 characters per prompt**, and Jackbox released at least 11 official themed episodes as shareable codes ([Steam community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/434170/discussions/0/305509857561889566/); [Jackboxpedia Q2](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_2)). The adaptation should do four things. It should track prompts seen per room and per returning device, so prompts are drawn without repetition. It should launch with a large base pool, on the order of 1,000. It should ship themed packs (ER, OR, nursing school, dental clinic, boards season, match day). And it should support custom prompt packs shared by code, which a residency program or nursing cohort would use to build inside jokes. The 15-minute game is a natural "one more round" unit, but no telemetry on session length or games per session exists.

The competitive landscape points to an open niche. Free browser party games such as Gartic Phone are ad-funded with no paywall. One reviewer saw a single ~30-second ad before a round, then "the rest of the game ran ad-free" ([WhistleOut](https://www.whistleout.com/CellPhones/Apps/gartic-phone-app-review)). One ad network markets inventory across Gartic Phone and skribbl.io ([AdinPlay](https://adinplay.com)). Hobby open-source Quiplash clones and phone-controller frameworks exist, such as [quiplash-js](https://www.github.com/simondiep/quiplash-js) and [phoneparty](https://github.com/shone/phoneparty), but their licenses and maintenance are unverified and most look stale. Medical humor, meanwhile, sells only as physical, gift-oriented card games: *DiagnosTHIS* (4.3/5 on 24 reviews, bought as nurse graduation gifts) ([Target](https://www.target.com/p/diagnos-this-card-game/-/A-94769181)), *What Do You Meme? Nurses Edition*, *Quack in the Box*, and *Code Brown*, the latter billed as the first card game for nurses and now discontinued ([Trusted Health](https://www.trustedhealth.com/code-brown)). The research found **no digital, phone-controller medical party game**. A free browser game would fill that gap. A pre-round ad or institutional sponsorship (schools, residency programs, student associations) fits the free-to-play model, though sponsorship as a business model has not been tested here.

## Copy the rules, invent everything players can see or hear

US copyright law leaves the Quiplash loop free to use. 17 U.S.C. § 102(b) excludes any "idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation" from protection ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102)). The Copyright Office's games guidance says copyright "does not protect the idea for a game, its name or title, or the method or methods for playing it," while the text of the rules and the artwork can be protected ([FL-108, archived](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20170124184513/https://www.copyright.gov/fls/fl108.pdf)). The closest precedent is *DaVinci Editrice v. ZiKo Games*, a competitive social card game with no fixed narrative. There, shared rules and method of play could not support infringement, and the defendants won summary judgment in 2016 because art and theme differed ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txsdce/4:2013cv03415/1134359/73/)). *Data East v. Epyx* likewise limited protection to the original expressive contributions, such as specific scoreboard and background design ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_East_USA,_Inc._v._Epyx,_Inc.)). The other side of the line is just as clear. In *Tetris v. Xio*, the court held that the idea of the game was free but the design of its components was protected. It found infringement for near-identical visual copying and protected the pieces' "arbitrary flourishes" as trade dress ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2012/06/tetris-holding-llc-v-xio-interactive-inc)). *Spry Fox v. 6waves* showed that a reskin swapping bears for yetis survives a motion to dismiss when progression and expressive particulars are copied closely. That case settled with the clone's IP transferred to Spry Fox ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spry_Fox,_LLC_v._Lolapps,_Inc.)). A medical theme alone does not make a close copy safe. The new expression has to be substantial.

| Safe to borrow (rules and methods) | Must be original or avoided |
|---|---|
| Private written answers, then two anonymous answers shown side by side, then vote | The names Quiplash, Jackbox, Fibbage, Last Lash, Thriplash, Safety Quip, and any "-lash"/"quip-"/"Jack-"/"-box" construction |
| Vote-share scoring, escalating round multipliers, sweep bonus, jinx, half-point fallback answer | Schmitty or any lookalike unseen-host presentation; host lines and voice |
| Three-answer finale; acronym, comic, or word-insertion final formats | Every prompt: no transcribing, sampling, or one-to-one paraphrasing of Jackbox prompt lists |
| Phone-browser controller, shared host screen, room codes, audience mode | Screen layouts, the "VS" vote composition, color palette, typography, join-page look, room-code banner style, animation timing |
| Fill-in-the-blank and other generic joke frames | Art, claymation-style characters, music, stings, sound effects |
| Medical facts, condition names, generic (INN) drug names | NHS logos and the NHS lozenge, NHS prose (unless licensed under the OGL with attribution), brand drug names, real hospitals, living people |

**Trademarks.** QUIPLASH is a registered US word mark (reg. 7425654, registered June 2024) owned by Jackbox Games ([Gleanmark](https://gleanmark.com/trademark/uspto-97884785)), as is FIBBAGE (reg. 4855109) ([Markinton](https://markinton.com/trademark/fibbage-86590012)). These registrations come from aggregators and should be confirmed on USPTO TSDR. No federal filing was found for DRAWFUL or LAST LASH, but common-law rights still apply. Saying "inspired by party games like Quiplash" in an FAQ is likely defensible as nominative fair use: use only as much of the mark as necessary and imply no endorsement ([Playboy v. Welles](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/279/796/506072); [Wikipedia: Nominative use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominative_use)). Keep the mark out of the title, logo, domain, app-store keywords and ad bids, and add a "not affiliated with Jackbox Games" line. Being free to play gives no shelter, because Lanham Act exposure turns on use in commerce and confusion, not on profit.

**Prompts.** Short individual prompts are probably uncopyrightable "words and short phrases" under 37 C.F.R. § 202.1(a) ([Bitlaw](https://www.bitlaw.com/source/37cfr/202_1.html)), even when "novel or distinctive" ([Copyright Office Review Board](https://www.Copyright.gov/rulings-filings/review-board/docs/rub-dirt.pdf)). Jackbox's prompt library as a whole, however, is a protectable compilation of creative selection and arrangement under § 103 ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/103)), and many prompts are not short. Fan wikis host large prompt lists, which makes copying tempting; resist it. Keep an authorship log (writer, date, origin) for every prompt as evidence of independent creation. Screen any AI-drafted prompts against known Jackbox prompts, because a model can reproduce memorized lines.

**Medical content.** Condition names and medical facts are free to use. NHS.uk text is reusable under the Open Government Licence v3.0 with the attribution "Contains public sector information licensed under the Open Government Licence v3.0" ([NHS.uk terms](https://www.nhs.uk/our-policies/terms-and-conditions/)). The OGL excludes logos, third-party material, trademarks and personal data, and forbids implying official status or endorsement ([SPDX OGL-UK-3.0](https://spdx.org/licenses/OGL-UK-3.0.html)). The re-user must obtain any third-party consents ([National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/legal/copyright/crown-copyright-and-our-website/)). NHS inform's own terms could not be retrieved, so the safest course is to write all medical flavor text from scratch and never use NHS branding. Long-dead figures like Semmelweis or Galen are low-risk targets for parody. Living or recently deceased doctors are not, given post-mortem publicity rights of up to 70 years in California and 40 in New York ([Fish & Richardson](https://www.fr.com/news-insights/articles/new-york-passes-post-mortem-right-of-publicity-law/)). Use fictional characters instead. A "not medical advice" disclaimer is prudent for any health-adjacent product.

**Fonts and patents.** Serving a font via @font-face distributes the file to every visitor. Demo fonts typically say "ONLY allowed for PERSONAL USE / NO COMMERCIAL USE" ([cdnfonts](https://www.cdnfonts.com/caliyandemo.font)), and desktop and web licences are separate products ([TypeTogether](https://www.type-together.com/understanding-webfont-licensing)). Web embedding has to be granted explicitly, as some free licences do ([Font Squirrel](https://www.fontsquirrel.com/fonts/Daela?q=Amatic)). Use only fonts whose licence permits web embedding, such as open-licensed families, and assume that ads or donations count as commercial use. No patents assigned to Jackbox turned up ([Arapacke Law](https://arapackelaw.com/software-patents-esports-companies/)), and phone-as-controller with room codes has ample prior art. A professional freedom-to-operate search was not performed, and the UK/EU positions were not verified in this research.

## Conclusion

Quiplash is a set of small structural decisions in support of the joke. There are exactly two answers per prompt, so comparing them takes no effort. Authors stay anonymous until the room has laughed. Scoring is by share, so crowd size never distorts it. There is a penalty for the obvious answer and a bonus for the answer everyone agrees on. A narrator makes sure the room never waits in silence. Players stay for those decisions, and they leave over what surrounds them: stale prompt pools, weak finales, missing custom content, and moderation added long after launch. A medical adaptation can therefore improve on the original rather than merely copy it. It can launch with a Thriplash-style dueling finale, a published and capped rule for audience weighting, room codes that resist brute force, filtering of answers and names by default, and a prompt pool tiered by audience breadth, tracked so it never repeats, and extensible with cohort-specific custom packs.

For this audience, the deciding question is breadth more than edginess. Every medical-humor product so far has been a card deck that clinicians love and outsiders do not understand. Most of its prompts should aim at the shared pain of training and the system (pagers, charting, rounds, boards), which nursing, PA, dental and medical students all recognize, rather than at patients or specialty trivia. Anonymity lets the hierarchy flatten for fifteen minutes. If the original host, art, sound and prompt bank keep the loop clearly separate from Jackbox's expression, the game is legally distinct as well as different in feel, and in a field with no digital medical party game, it would have the space mostly to itself.
