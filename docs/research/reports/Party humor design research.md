# Party humor design research

> Question: What makes party-game content genuinely funny and fun for players with ANY sense of humor (dry, absurd, wholesome, dark-ish, punny, gross-out, observational), across ages and backgrounds, applied to a medical-themed browser party game that works for laypeople (Normal mode) and healthcare workers (Pro mode)? Research specifically: (1) prompt design for Quiplash-style fill-in-the-blank / open-ended comedy prompts: which prompt structures produce the widest range of funny answers (specificity, constraints, absurd premises, personal/inside-joke prompts, open vs closed), with evidence from Jackbox writers, improv theory and comedy-writing craft; (2) party trivia design that stays fun rather than test-like: question types that create laughs and table talk (surprising facts, 'would you believe' history, wrong-answer humor, gross-out body facts, myth vs fact), difficulty curves, fun distractor writing, and how games like Trivia Murder Party, Fibbage, HQ Trivia and pub quizzes keep it entertaining; (3) drawing/guessing game design (Drawful, Gartic Phone, Pictionary, skribbl.io): word selection that makes drawings funny, ambiguity, the sweet spot of difficulty, hint pacing, and why bad drawings are funny; (4) game-mode variety for mixed groups and large parties (up to 40 people, families, older adults): team formats, quick rounds, low-skill participation, and what keeps everyone engaged; (5) humor theory relevant to mixed audiences (benign violation theory, incongruity, superiority), what makes healthcare/medical humor land with outsiders versus insiders, and how to give room for edgier humor without the game itself pushing anyone. Cite sources and prefer primary designer interviews, GDC talks, and peer-reviewed humor research.

## Summary

The evidence that survived verification points to one main rule: party content is funniest when it is a benign violation. That means it breaks an expectation in an obviously safe, playful frame. Content that is only shocking or only safe gets fewer laughs (McGraw & Warren 2010, 2012; peer-reviewed but contested). For open-ended comedy prompts, Jackbox's own writers recommend four things: (1) add one specific, absurd constraint, not several; (2) check you can think of several answers to your own prompt; (3) mix prompt structures and the kind of answer each asks for; (4) use silly words, imaginary characters and relatable embarrassing situations to signal that whimsy is welcome. For drawing modes, the funny parts are absurd compound prompts (for example, Napoleon riding a squirrel), deliberately crude tools (no eraser, so it's 'Dr-AWFUL'), and a second writing layer where players bluff decoy titles for each drawing. For large mixed groups, the best-supported pattern is tiers: a small core of players plus an audience that votes and swings the result, with people rotating in between games. For the Normal/Pro split, the research suggests two things. Insiders and outsiders find different jokes funny depending on how strongly they hold the norm being broken. Clinician 'blame the patient' humor (obesity, self-inflicted conditions) is a known kind of punching down that may read as mean, not funny. Severe medical outcomes work better framed as distant or hypothetical, and small bodily mishaps work better framed as close and personal. Two parts of the question have almost no surviving evidence: making trivia fun rather than test-like, and team formats for groups up to 40.

## Verified findings

### Prompt specificity: adding one specific, absurd constraint to a vague prompt makes answers much better, but stacking several constraints makes it worse. 'An erotic movie' is too vague, 'an erotic movie for elephants' is ideal, and 'a 1940s erotic film for elephants at the St. Louis Zoo' is too much. Aim for a balance.

- Confidence: **medium** · Vote: 3-0
- Evidence: Spencer Ham (Jackbox writer), Jackbox blog, 2016: 'Be specific whenever possible ... way too much info to process ... Find the right balance.' Vote 3-0. This is a designer's own craft advice, not empirical testing.
- Sources: https://www.jackboxgames.com/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt

### Prompt quality test and variety: a good prompt should suggest many quick answers, and if the writer can't come up with a good answer, the prompt probably fails. A prompt set should mix structures (fill-in-the-blank, questions, statements) and answer types (puns, phrases, questions, objects), so players aren't set up for the same kind of joke every round.

- Confidence: **medium** · Vote: 3-0 (two merged claims)
- Evidence: Same Jackbox post: 'If you can't think of a good answer to your own prompt, then it's probably not going to be very good'; 'Don't just write fill-in-the-blanks--mix it up with prompts in the form of questions and statements'; 'don't set-up people with the same type of joke each time.' Two claims merged, both voted 3-0.
- Sources: https://www.jackboxgames.com/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt

### Setting the tone and sourcing material: writers deliberately use silly words, imaginary characters and absurd premises (for example, 'What the boogeyman is afraid of') to show new players that whimsical answers are welcome. They also turn their own embarrassing real experiences into 'how could this have gone worse' prompts about familiar situations. This combination gives people with different senses of humor something to work with: absurd, wholesome-silly and observational/cringe.

- Confidence: **medium** · Vote: 3-0 (two merged claims)
- Evidence: C.J. Tuor, Jackbox blog, 2019: 'by introducing silly phrases, imaginary characters and ridiculous premises, we hope new players understand that their response should be equally whimsical'; turning 'a particularly embarrassing Valentine's Day memory' into a prompt so players can 'ponder how it could have gone worse.' Personal experience is one source among several; others include old textbooks and past games. Two claims merged, both voted 3-0.
- Sources: https://www.jackboxgames.com/the-science-of-creating-a-quiplash-prompt/

### Benign-violation theory, the main framework: humor happens when something is seen as a violation and as benign at the same moment. Too much threat stops it from feeling benign; too little threat means there's no violation. Content that is only shocking, or only safe, should get fewer laughs. For a medical game, the job is to break an expectation (gross, taboo, absurd) inside an obviously playful frame.

- Confidence: **high** · Vote: 2-1 and 3-0 (merged duplicates)
- Evidence: McGraw & Warren 2010 (Psychological Science): 'three conditions are jointly necessary and sufficient ... violation ... benign ... simultaneously.' McGraw et al. 2012: 'either too much or too little threat impedes humor.' Both peer-reviewed and primary. The authors' 2021 review softens 'necessary and sufficient' to 'help distinguish' humorous from non-humorous experiences. Critics raise falsifiability concerns, and play-mirth theory (2024) is a rival account. Two duplicate claims merged (votes 2-1 and 3-0).
- Sources: https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf, https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.williams.leonard.2012.pdf

### Insider versus outsider humor: how strongly someone holds the norm being broken changes whether they find it funny, even when everyone is equally disgusted. Non-churchgoers and churchgoers were about equally disgusted by a church raffling off a Hummer (69% vs 65%), but non-churchgoers were amused far more often (92% vs 62%). Applied to Normal/Pro modes: jokes that break a medical norm clinicians hold strongly may land worse with clinicians than with laypeople. Content about clinical culture's own norms may need tuning separately for each mode.

- Confidence: **medium** · Vote: 3-0
- Evidence: McGraw & Warren 2010, Study 4: amused 92% vs 62%, χ2(1, N≈39)=4.1, p<.05; disgust 69% vs 65%, not significant. The sample is small (about 13 non-churchgoers) and commitment was only measured, not manipulated. Most insiders were still amused (62%), and the reverse case (insiders amused, outsiders not) was never tested.
- Sources: https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf

### How to frame severity: across five studies, severe violations (tragedies) were rated less unfunny when distant in time, socially, hypothetically or in space, and mild mishaps were funnier when close. For game content, frame serious medical outcomes as distant, fictional or hypothetical (historical medicine, absurd scenarios). Frame small everyday bodily mishaps (sneezing, hiccups, stubbed toes, embarrassing sounds) as close and personal.

- Confidence: **medium** · Vote: 3-0
- Evidence: McGraw, Warren, Williams & Leonard 2012 (Psychological Science 23(10)) found a significant severity × distance crossover in all five studies. The severe-is-funnier-when-distant effect alone was weak in two studies (p=.14, p=.06). Severe material stayed barely funny even when distant (mean about 0.9, versus 1.3–1.7 for mild). Franchin et al. 2023 found no distance effect for moral violations in a different paradigm. A related claim, that distance raises amusement without lowering disgust, was refuted 1-2.
- Sources: https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.williams.leonard.2012.pdf

### Medical punching down: in focus groups, medical students said the patients most often joked about were those seen as 'fair game': obese patients and patients whose conditions were seen as preventable or self-inflicted (smokers, heavy drinkers, drug users, noncompliant patients). Insider 'blame the patient' jokes are a known superiority-humor pattern, and laypeople, who are themselves patients, may find them mean rather than funny. Safer insider material targets the system, the job, the paperwork or the clinicians themselves.

- Confidence: **medium** · Vote: 2-1
- Evidence: Wear et al. 2006, Academic Medicine 81(5):454-462, a qualitative study of 58 third- and fourth-year students in five focus groups. Its first theme: 'patients ... deemed fair game due to obesity or other conditions perceived as preventable or self-inflicted.' A follow-up study with residents and attendings found similar categories. 'Most often' overstates it: this is a leading theme, not a counted ranking. How outsiders react was not tested. A related claim, that this humor is mainly stress relief for insiders, was refuted 0-3.
- Sources: https://scilit.com/publications/7d9bbeb0af771df8cf27720690cc1735

### What makes drawing modes funny: Drawful uses absurd, specific compound prompts (for example, 'Napoleon riding a squirrel') instead of single nouns. It deliberately limits the tools (drawing on a phone, no eraser) so the art comes out funny and bad, and the game doesn't turn into a drawing-skill contest. After each drawing is revealed, players anonymously write decoy titles to fool others, Fibbage-style. That adds a second writing layer where non-artists can score.

- Confidence: **high** · Vote: 3-0 (three merged claims)
- Evidence: The Drawful product page: 'absurd things like Napoleon riding a squirrel'; 'draw on their mobile device (without the aid of an eraser)'; 'fooling other players into selecting your decoy answer'. The Jackbox survey response: no eraser because 'It's called Dr-AWFUL for a reason ... Sometimes the drawing error is part of the fun' and the team doesn't want these games 'to always feel like a drawing competition.' Three claims merged, all voted 3-0. The 'historical figure plus animal' pattern is one example, not a confirmed rule.
- Sources: https://jackboxgames.com/drawful, https://www.jackboxgames.com/ (Responding to Your Questions from the Jackbox Customer Survey, Nov 2022, cited by verifier)

### Large and mixed groups, tiered participation: Quiplash has 3-8 core players plus an optional audience of up to 10,000 that votes and changes the outcome, described as an 'enhanced spectator' role. In a party bigger than the cap, the extras join the audience and can rotate in for the next game. This is the best-supported pattern for groups of up to 40: low-effort voting keeps everyone involved without everyone having to write.

- Confidence: **high** · Vote: 3-0 (two merged claims)
- Evidence: PlayStation Blog 2015 (Jackbox guest post): 'played by 3-8 core players ... The Quiplash Audience actually affects the outcome of the game by voting'; 'an enhanced spectator role ... If you have a party of 10 people, 2 can be in the Audience.' Jackbox product pages confirm the figures. Two claims merged, both voted 3-0. 'Keeps big parties engaged' is the stated design intent, not a measured result.
- Sources: https://blog.playstation.com/2015/06/30/quiplash-new-party-game-expands-audience-participation-to-10000/, https://jackboxgames.com/quiplash

### Where funny content comes from: many Jackbox writers come from Chicago's improv scene, and the CCO credits that background for the 'outrageous' writing. Many Dirty Drawful prompts were taken from what came up in the studio's own playtests. The takeaway is to generate and refine prompts through live playtesting with real groups.

- Confidence: **medium** · Vote: 3-0 (two merged claims)
- Evidence: Allard Laban (Jackbox CCO), Gamereactor interview, 2024: 'A lot of our writers come from the improv community in Chicago'; 'Especially in Dirty Drawful, we harvest a lot of the prompts from our actual playtesting.' A 2025 NPR/Here & Now interview with Brooke Breit backs up the improv connection ('yes, and'). Two claims merged, both voted 3-0. These are secondary outlets reporting direct executive quotes, and the executives are promoting their own company.
- Sources: https://www.gamereactor.eu/the-jackbox-naughty-pack-was-inspired-by-the-dirty-minds-of-the-players-1430943/

### Content for every age: Jackbox's Hear Say creative director says broad testing made the content work 'for six and sixty year olds alike,' with about 95% of its content family friendly. This supports defaulting to broadly accessible content and treating edgier material as a small minority.

- Confidence: **low** · Vote: 3-0
- Evidence: Alina Constantin, interview 2025: 'tested extensively to make the content accessible to as many as possible ... played very successfully for six and sixty year olds alike.' This is the developer's own anecdotal judgment with no published playtest data. The related claim that the edgier remainder is tagged and opt-in was refuted 0-3.
- Sources: https://lootlevelchill.com/?p=8040

### Trivia pacing: HQ Trivia deliberately swung question difficulty between trivially easy and obscure. TIME argues these swings between reward and disappointment felt almost like gambling and kept players hooked. For party trivia, a sawtooth difficulty pattern (easy wins scattered among hard surprises) is likely to feel less like a test than a steady upward climb.

- Confidence: **low** · Vote: 3-0
- Evidence: TIME, 2018: questions range 'from mind-numbingly easy to the esoteric and obscure ... engineered that way'; 'These wild swings between reward and disappointment make playing HQ almost feel like gambling.' The gambling comparison is the journalist's opinion, and HQ closed in 2020. Two claims about HQ distractor writing were refuted 0-3.
- Sources: https://time.com/5189381/hq-trivia-app-questions/

## caveats

Source mix: the prompt-craft and drawing findings come almost entirely from Jackbox's own blog posts, product pages and interviews. That makes them primary designer testimony, but it is promotional, self-described and not empirically tested. There were no GDC talks, no independent playtest data, and no peer-reviewed work on game design itself among the surviving claims.

Humor theory: benign-violation theory is peer-reviewed but contested. It has falsifiability critiques, its authors softened the 'necessary and sufficient' wording in 2021, a 2024 rival theory exists, and a 2023 study found no distance effect for moral violations. The underlying studies (2010, 2012) are small, pre-replication-crisis undergraduate and MTurk samples. Treat them as design heuristics, not laws.

Medical humor: the evidence is a single 2006 qualitative study at one US medical school. Nothing was tested on how laypeople react to clinician humor.

Coverage gaps: almost nothing survived on making trivia fun rather than test-like. Myth-vs-fact, gross body facts, wrong-answer humor, distractor writing, and pub quiz, Trivia Murder Party and Fibbage mechanics were all missing, and both HQ distractor claims were refuted. Nothing survived on Gartic Phone, skribbl.io or Pictionary word difficulty and hint pacing, on team formats beyond audience voting, or on older-adult accessibility. Puns, dry humor and gross-out humor as separate styles were not addressed empirically.

Content filtering: the user wants to remove the slur/curse blocker. No surviving claim directly addresses content filtering. The two Jackbox-related claims about opt-in edgy content and group-controlled moderation were refuted (0-3 and 1-2), so this research neither supports nor argues against removing the blocker. The theory findings bear on what the game's own written content should push. They don't bear on policing what players type.

Time sensitivity: HQ closed in 2020, and the Jackbox craft posts date from 2016-2019. Craft advice changes slowly, but specific product features (eraser, audience caps) may differ across newer titles.

## openQuestions

- Which trivia formats (myth vs fact, 'would you believe' history, gross body facts, joke wrong answers, Fibbage-style player-written lies) produce the most laughs and table talk with lay and clinician audiences, and how should funny distractors be written? None of the evidence on this survived verification.
- For drawing and guessing modes, what word difficulty and hint-reveal pacing (as in skribbl.io or Gartic Phone) hits the sweet spot? Does the absurd compound prompt style in Drawful transfer to medical terms without turning into a vocabulary test for laypeople?
- Past the audience-voting model, which team or round formats keep groups of 20-40 (families, older adults, low-tech players) engaged? Examples: team captains, shared-device teams, lightning rounds, or everyone-votes-everyone-answers.
- With no word filter, how far does the game's own content and framing (silly premises, distant or hypothetical framing of severe outcomes, avoiding blame-the-patient setups) shape the tone of what players write? Does that differ between Normal and Pro groups?
