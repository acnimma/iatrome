# Open every prompt, then stage the laugh

iatrome's five modes become Jackbox-quality through three disciplines applied the same way everywhere, not through more content. First, every prompt (written, drawn or debated) should supply a vivid, ordinary medical scene and leave the joke slot empty, so a dry room, a gross-out room and a wholesome room can each fill the same gap their own way. Second, every mode should run on one presentation system: a fixed 16:9 stage frame, shared type, space and duration tokens, one answer card component, and a reveal "beat clock" in which nothing new moves until the last laugh has landed. Third, every mode needs a finale that changes the *structure* of play rather than the *skill* being tested, plus a running social gag (a stigma, a stamp, a public revive) that turns scores into stories. The medical layer works when **the system is the villain and the patient is never the target**. That rule is also what lets laypeople and clinicians laugh at the same prompt, because insurers, pagers, gowns and hold music are hated from both sides of the exam table. The largest gaps today are concrete. Sketch draws from 234 single-noun terms that admit one canonical drawing. Survive the Shift resolves elimination as silent arithmetic and gives ghosts no path to win. Pick Your Poison pays a flat 100 points for picking the majority side. Didactics has no wager and no reveal theatre. Grand Rounds' all-play finale crowds the screen at 8 players. One caveat governs the visual recommendations: Jackbox has never published typography specs, reveal timings or timer lengths, so every duration and size below is a sourced principle turned into a starting value for playtesting, not a copied number.

## Prompts should supply the scene and leave the joke empty

Jackbox's published writing guidance is brief, and it converges on one idea: a prompt is a launch pad, not a joke. Spencer Ham's rules are that the writer must be able to answer their own prompt, that a good prompt "lends itself to a lot of quick ideas," and that specificity has a sweet spot. "The title of an erotic movie" is too vague, "...for elephants" works, and adding a decade and a zoo is "way too much info to process" ([Jackbox blog, Ham](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt)). Ham also warns against monotony ("Don't just write fill-in-the blanks") and against niche references, because **"if you're alienating even 1-2 people, the game is a lot less fun"** (same source). C.J. Tuor's six prompt sources (absurd premises, past mistakes, event-specific prompts, change-the-world prompts, butt jokes, and things writers sincerely want to know) amount to a tonal palette, and she insists a good prompt "should work with a variety of audiences" ([Jackbox blog, Tuor](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/the-science-of-creating-a-quiplash-prompt)). The only documented quality process is live playtesting. Jackbox harvests prompts from its own playtests ([Gamereactor](https://www.gamereactor.eu/the-jackbox-naughty-pack-was-inspired-by-the-dirty-minds-of-the-players-1430943/)), and its CEO concedes a staff of comedy writers produces "a lot of false positives" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/is-the-game-funny-or-just-the-players-jackbox-ceo-says-they-get-a-lot-of-false-positives-with-a-staff-of-comedy-writers/)).

Improv theory explains why some prompts let every sense of humor play. A scene is funny when a believable **base reality** meets a **first unusual thing**, which is then heightened, and the classic mistake is "making your first unusual thing TOO unusual" ([Hoopla Impro](https://www.hooplaimpro.com/quick-guide-game-of-scene)). Ham's overloaded elephant prompt fails because the writer has already spent the unusual-thing slot and the heightening, which leaves players one move: echo the prompt's joke. A prompt that "incubates" many humor styles is therefore a normal, vivid situation with a gap. The absurdist fills the gap with a category error, the deadpan player with understatement, the dark player with raised stakes, the wholesome player with a sweet twist, the nerd with precise jargon, and the twelve-year-old with the body. This maps onto benign-violation theory, where laughter needs a violation that is simultaneously read as safe ([McGraw & Warren 2010](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf)). The prompt supplies the safe frame, and each player chooses the size of the violation. The same logic governs ratings. Jackbox's family filter removes Jackbox's own adult premises but cannot touch player text, and a fan-relayed figure puts Quiplash XL at **844 normal prompts versus 154 adult ones**, roughly 15% ([Steam community thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/351510/discussions/0/594820656473105270), attribution to Jackbox unverified). The practical lesson is that a clean prompt with an open gap lets an adult room go blue and a family room go silly, while a dirty premise locks every room into one angle. Jackbox's Naughty Pack director calls the target tone "funcomfortable," everyone "in on the joke," with sensitivity reviews so content is "never trying to punch down" ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/141077/jackbox-naughty-pack-m-rated-interview)).

No Jackbox source documents a range test, so iatrome should adopt one as its own house method. The table below turns the cited principles into a review gate that a writer can run in a minute.

| Check | Pass condition | Failure it catches |
|---|---|---|
| Persona range | Distinct, fun answers from at least 4 of 6 personas (literal nerd, absurdist, deadpan cynic, wholesome, dark-but-distant, twelve-year-old) | Prompts with one tone ([Tuor](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/the-science-of-creating-a-quiplash-prompt)) |
| Jinx test | No two persona answers are near-identical | Obvious-answer gravity, like "worst thing your surgeon could say" collapsing to "Oops" |
| Self-answer | Writer can produce 3+ answers they would actually submit | Unanswerable prompts ([Ham](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/how-to-write-a-good-quiplash-prompt)) |
| One breath | Reads aloud in one breath, roughly 12-15 words (a heuristic, not a Jackbox number) | Overload |
| Three depths | Answerable by a layperson, a student and a clinician | Knowledge gates that alienate 1-2 players |
| Premise is not the punchline | No answer you can write is funnier than the prompt | Prompts that are already the joke |
| Target | The butt is a system, object, institution or clinician, never a patient group | Punching down |
| Structure budget | No sentence frame appears more than twice in one session | Template fatigue |

The structure budget matters more than topic variety. Format prompts ("X's out-of-office reply," "the real reason Y") teach players a single joke shape, and they start answering on autopilot. A quick count of iatrome's 350 Normal quips found about a dozen that open with "The real reason" and about twenty that open with some form of "worst." That is not excessive across a pool, but the session draw should enforce a per-frame cap so a single game never serves the same frame three times. Varying the *answer type* is the other lever Ham names: phrases, objects, names, titles and questions as answers. Six distinct archetypes cover the range. Inverted perspective ("what the exam table paper thinks about you") is Tuor's Boogeyman move. A pain-into-art scenario puts a gap at the worst moment of a shared indignity. A sincere question has no single answer. An imaginary-institution fact asks players to explain something fictional. A noun or title answer suits puns without the prompt itself punning. A question-as-answer asks what someone secretly wants to ask.

### Sketch's flat nouns leave nothing to misread

Drawing prompts have to do two jobs at once. Drawful's prompts are "straight-up bonkers" compound scenes, and Ham's sample is "three raccoons leaving a broom closet": a count, a creature, an action and a place ([Jackbox blog, Ham Q&A](https://www.jackboxgames.com/blog/mini-qa-with-drawful-animate-game-director-spencer-ham)). Drawful's scoring pays the artist **1,000 points per player who guesses correctly** while liars earn 500 per player fooled, and picking the game's own decoy costs 500 ([Jackboxpedia: Drawful](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Drawful)). The artist is therefore paid to draw clearly while liars hunt for alternate readings, and that tension only exists if the prompt allows both. The no-eraser rule is deliberate ("It's called Dr-AWFUL for a reason"), so the game doesn't become a drawing competition ([Jackbox: Drawful](https://jackboxgames.com/drawful)). Reviewers credit the limited palette and missing eraser for much of the fun ([TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/drawful-2-review-much)).

iatrome's Grand Rounds doodle pack already follows this pattern ("An octopus getting a flu shot in every arm"). Sketch's 234 Normal terms do not. They are single nouns such as "stethoscope," "tooth" and "heart," each with one canonical drawing. Everyone draws the same thing, guessers either get it in three seconds or never, and bad art has no comic payoff. The fix is a template: **a drawable subject, one relationship verb, and a place or object it interacts with**. Examples in house voice include "a skeleton waiting for its own X-ray results," "a pigeon taking a blood pressure," and "a pager trying to fall asleep." Each noun should be sketchable by a non-artist in under ten seconds, abstract nouns (burnout, anxiety) should live in the verb rather than the subject, and the prompt must never require written letters, since drawers writing words on the canvas is skribbl.io's chronic failure mode ([Mechanics of Magic](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2024/04/24/critical-play-scribbl-io-2/)). Compound prompts are harder to type exactly, so Sketch's guess matcher should accept keyword-level matches ("skeleton" plus "x-ray") rather than the full phrase. The PP10 team's note "NO MORE PIZZA DRAWINGS" is the warning for any loose drawing prompt: without curation, open prompts converge on defaults ([Jackbox blog, PP10 directing](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-pp10-directing)).

## Medicine's funniest villain is the system, never the patient

The two-audience problem is real and documented. Medical card games have repeatedly failed outsiders. *Quack in the Box* is "really funny for those in the medical field more than for outsiders" ([GeekDad](https://geekdad.com/2011/04/quack-in-the-box-makes-medical-malpractice-into-a-game/)), and a reviewer's non-medical husband "didn't get the jokes" in a nurses' edition party game ([Lattes & Literature](https://lattesandliterature.com/products/what-do-you-meme-career-series-nurses)). The crossover successes share a structure. Dr. Glaucomflecken (ophthalmologist Will Flanary) built his following on insurance sketches. In about 90 seconds a reasonable person confronts an absurd system, and his targets are "institutions preying on people who are less powerful than them," medical training and his own overpaid field ([STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2022/03/25/dr-glaucomflecken-will-flanary-profile/)). His specialty stereotypes draw on idiosyncrasies most laypeople have never heard of, yet commentators credit him with "explaining some complex things clearly and humorously" for a broad audience ([Freakonomics](https://freakonomics.com/podcast/the-comedian-ophthalmologist-will-see-you-now)). Adam Kay's *This Is Going to Hurt* sold over two million copies with wry anecdotes about a system that strains junior doctors ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/This_Is_Going_to_Hurt)). The BMJ Christmas issue models the deadpan register: rigorous method applied to a silly question, with editors chasing "strangeness" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/12/25/1198908975/christmas-bmj-health-research)).

Flanary's own rule is the cleanest guardrail for a mixed table: make the object of ridicule clear, ridicule only what you believe deserves it, and never be perceived as making fun of patients, because **"patients are at the bottom"** ([STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2022/03/25/dr-glaucomflecken-will-flanary-profile/)). The clinical literature draws the same line by audience and target. In a survey of 710 New Zealand mental health staff, 79.4% called black humour beneficial, but respondents located harm in humour at a patient's expense, humour that trivializes distress, and humour aimed at religion, culture or family. Benefit ratings were lower among Māori and Pasifika staff (66.0%) and non-native English speakers (67.7%) ([PMC10809729](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809729/)). Paramedics call their humour "appropriately inappropriate for the correct audience" and "not funny for non-medical professionals" ([PMC11610543](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11610543/)). Medical students describe obese and "self-inflicted" patients as "fair game" ([Wear et al. 2006](https://scilit.com/publications/7d9bbeb0af771df8cf27720690cc1735)), a pattern an accompanying editorial framed as dehumanization ([Berk](https://www.ronberk.com/articles/2009_derogatory.pdf)). A party game is an outsider audience by definition, even in Pro mode, because partners, roommates and first-year students are in the room. The game's own content should therefore stay at the safe end of insider humor (self, specialty, system, absurd tasks) and leave darker material to whatever players choose to type. One honest gap remains: **no study has measured how laypeople react to clinician humor directly**, so the line between Normal and Pro is a design judgment that playtests must validate.

The richest shared material is the patient-as-underdog experience. About 27% of US adults admit lying to their dentist about flossing, and the periodontists' reaction was that the figure seemed low ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2015/06/24/417184367/are-you-flossing-or-just-lying-about-flossing-the-dentist-knows)). "Universal small lies" like this are ideal prompt bait because the player confesses on their own behalf and nobody is mocked. Pharmacy's "sassy vs polite pharmacist" format, which answers the same customer question two ways, is a native two-answer comedy structure ([Pharmacy Times](https://www.pharmacytimes.com/view/sassy-pharmacist-and-polite-pharmacist-tackle-common-questions)). Clinicians praising *The Pitt* for crowding, boarding and the charting burden TV usually omits shows that 2026 insider humor is again system-focused ([NPR](https://npr.org/2026/01/08/nx-s1-5671156/pitt-season-2-tv-show-medicine)). Patient malapropisms are a collected clinician genre, but linguists note they occur regardless of education and can signal anxiety ([Visual Thesaurus](https://www.visualthesaurus.com/cm/wordroutes/medical-misapprehensions)). The safe version aims the joke at medicine's *jargon* ("what 'slight pressure' actually means") rather than at the confused patient.

These findings sort into prompt territory that serves both audiences. The waiting room, the gown, the exam-table paper, the phone tree, the Explanation of Benefits that is "not a bill," prior authorization, hold music, hospital socks, 4 a.m. vitals and nine-hour discharges all land with families because the patient is the protagonist. They land with clinicians because clinicians fight the same machine. Pro mode should reference the insider *experience* (charting at home, consult wars, the Pyxis lockout, "clinical correlation recommended," specialty stereotypes) rather than Normal mode plus jargon, which lets a clinician's partner laugh at the gist. A promotion test follows from Kay's crossover: if a non-clinician gets the joke from the prompt alone, it belongs in both modes. Severity framing completes the guardrail. Across five studies, severe violations read as less unfunny when distant in time or hypothetical, while mild mishaps are funnier when close ([McGraw et al. 2012](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.williams.leonard.2012.pdf)). Death, cancer, addiction, weight, disability and mental illness stay out of prompts. Grim facts move to historical framing (leeches, Victorian quackery, tobacco smoke enemas), and hiccups, brain freeze and the crinkling gown stay close and personal. Patient slang ("gomer," "frequent flyer," "status dramaticus") never appears in game content, even as satire, and puns stay out of prompts because the house voice is deadpan. Players can still pun in their answers.

## Finales should change the structure, not the skill

Quiplash has shipped three finales, and each one fixed the last one's flaw. The original Last Lash had everyone answer one prompt, with **three stackable votes** per player, a 3,000-point pool split by vote share, and answers revealed **"with the least amount of votes first and the most, last"** ([Jackboxpedia: Quiplash](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash)). Quiplash 2's Last Lash rotated three constrained formats: Acro-Lash (three letters), Word-Lash (a forced word) and Comic-Lash (a blank speech bubble). Each player awarded gold, silver and bronze to three *different* answers ([Jackboxpedia: Quiplash 2](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_2)), and with 3-4 players only gold and silver were awarded ([Fandom](https://jackboxgames.fandom.com/wiki/Quiplash_(series))). Quiplash 3's Thriplash went back to head-to-head pairs in which each player writes three answers to one prompt, with **no safety quip**, points tripled to a reported maximum of 6,000 per prompt, and the host character Schmitty filling the odd seat against the lowest scorer ([Jackboxpedia: Quiplash 3](https://jackbox.wiki/wiki/Quiplash_3)). Thriplash belongs to Quiplash 3 (2020), not Quiplash 2. The record has holes. Quiplash 2's medal point values and reveal order are undocumented. Sources conflict on whether Last Lash audience members get one vote or three. Thriplash's line-by-line choreography appears in no text source and would need gameplay footage to confirm.

Player reception shows a pattern. Steam review mining lists a "disappointing Final Lash" among Quiplash 2's recurring complaints ([Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/1111940/quiplash_2_inter_las_hional/stats/details)), and one community comment argued that the three constrained formats are each "not particularly like Quiplash" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1211630/discussions/0/2952628643855845863)). Thriplash drew praise as "the best iteration of the game yet" ([Geek Outpost](https://geekoutpost.com/jackbox-party-pack-7-review/)). The finale that kept the core skill (open joke-writing) and raised stakes through quantity won. The finales that swapped in a different skill (acronym puzzles) lost. The design logic of the voting systems is also clear. Stackable votes let a decisive best answer take the pool and make the lowest-to-highest reveal a countdown to a champion. Forced medals spread points across more players and reduce kingmaking. Duels preserve the anonymity and versus contrast that make the core loop work ([Mechanics of Magic, Kula](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2023/04/30/critical-play-quiplash-judging-games-jonathan/)).

iatrome's current Grand Rounds finale, the Differential, is an all-play hybrid. Everyone writes a list of three to one prompt, every list is presented in sequence, and players vote for one list. A vote is worth 500 before a 4x round multiplier, with a 250 win bonus and a 500 sweep bonus. That is a sound structure at 3-5 players. At 8 players, though, the stage has to show 24 lines, the 25-second finale vote asks each voter to hold eight lists in their head, and the "too many cards" problem that Thriplash's duels avoid returns. The recommendation is to make the finale **count-adaptive**. At 3-5 players, keep the all-play Differential, add three stackable votes (no self-votes), and reveal lists from fewest votes to most so the last card lands as the winner. At 6-8 players, switch to Differential duels: pairs write lists of three, lines reveal alternately (A1, B1, A2, B2, A3, B3) so the third line works as the punchline, voters pick the better set, and Beep fills the odd seat against the last-place player, as Schmitty does. Either way the safety quip stays off in the finale. An acronym round ("three letters, invent the hospital committee") suits medicine's abbreviation culture, but it should be an occasional variant rather than the default, given the community verdict on constrained formats. Every phase should end early once all inputs are in, because a reviewer faulted the original Quiplash for vote timers that "doesn't automatically zero out once all the votes are cast" ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/90364/quiplash-review-just-add-people-and-lash-away)).

## One stage frame and one beat clock create cohesion

Jackbox's own art leads describe the cohesion model in one sentence: the games "share structure and general UI," and "the art sets them apart" ([Jackbox blog, PP10 art](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-pp10-art)). The same post supplies the hierarchy test, which asks whether "everything [is] competing for attention and confusing the players," and the principle that art should be striking "without distracting from the gameplay." Survey Scramble's director adds, "We never want to outshine the people actually playing" ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/gaming/the-jacbox-survey-scramble-interview/)). The Jack Principles underneath all of it are to maintain pacing with one task at a time, to create an illusion of awareness, and to borrow TV's "narrative timing, transitions, and dialogue" ([Built In](https://builtin.com/media-gaming/jackbox-games-design-party-pack)). For iatrome this means a single component system used identically by all five modes: one `AnswerCard` with states (idle, spotlight, dimmed, winner, jinx), one `PromptBanner`, one `Stamp` callout, one `ScoreTicker`, and a phone `VoteButton` whose letter chip and color match its stage card. Each mode varies only its accent color, background motif and stamp copy.

### Players' words are the largest, cleanest objects on screen

Every stage screen should have exactly one hero element. The prompt is the hero while players write. During a matchup the two answers take over and the prompt shrinks to a header. At the reveal the hero is the tally and then the winner, and during scoring it is the leaderboard. Room code, timer and host controls shrink or fade. Because players' text is the star, answer cards should carry the highest contrast and largest type. Decoration belongs on the *frame* (jelly gloss, ink outline, drop shadow, a slight tilt), never on the letterforms of long answers. This diagnoses the current flatness. A raised text shadow on Dogbangy gives depth to letters, but cards that are flat color without their own gloss, outline and shadow still read as flat. Jackbox-style depth lives on containers that sit above a vignetted background. The type split should be enforced by length rather than left to a comment. Dogbangy suits prompts up to about 90 characters, answers up to about 60, names, scores and callouts. Inter in heavy weights takes long case stems and any answer over the threshold. Prompts and answers stay in sentence case, and all caps is reserved for short callouts (round titles, room code, the unanimous stamp). On the phone, Jackbox locks the controller to a tiny vocabulary of drawing, choosing from buttons and entering text, so a player who learns one game knows them all ([Built In](https://builtin.com/media-gaming/jackbox-games-design-party-pack)). iatrome's equivalents are a prompt-plus-text-field card, a vertical stack of full-width vote buttons 48-56 px tall that grow to wrap rather than truncate, a waiting card that says what is happening on the TV ("Look up. Votes are in."), and the draw pad. Submit feedback should respond locally within 100 ms by echoing the submitted text, then show who is still writing as avatars grey in.

### A 16:9 safe frame ends "different on every screen"

The scattered one-off `clamp()` values in iatrome's CSS modules, each with its own viewport slope, are the main source of inconsistent sizing at in-between widths. `tokens.css` defines palette, radii, shadows and easings but no type or space scale. The fix has three parts. First, render the stage into a centered frame (`width: min(100vw, 177.78vh)`, `aspect-ratio: 16/9`, `container-type: size`) with a 5% safe inset, the margin TV platforms converge on: Roku asks for at least 5%, and Fire TV asks that text stay within the inner 90% ([Roku](https://developer.roku.com/dev/docs/key-design-principles); [Amazon Fire TV](https://developer.amazon.com/docs/fire-tv/design-and-user-experience-guidelines.html); [Android TV](https://developer.android.com/design/ui/tv/guides/foundations/design-for-tv)). A 720p TV, a 1440x900 laptop and an ultrawide then all get the same composition, and the vignette fills the letterbox. Second, size all stage type and space in container units of that frame (`cqi`), and size phone type with Utopia-style fluid steps, `clamp(min, rem + vi, max)` interpolated between a small and a large modular scale ([Smashing Magazine](https://smashingmagazine.com/2021/04/designing-developing-fluid-type-space-scales/); [Utopia](https://utopia.fyi/blog/clamp/)). Both families use the same step names, so "step 3" means the same role on the TV and on the phone, and the space scale derives from type step 0. Third, keep a rem term in phone clamps and cap max/min ratios at 2.5 so browser zoom still works ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/baseline-in-action-fluid-type); [Chrome guidance](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/modern-web-guidance/guides/css/fluid-scaling)). Pure `cqi` stage text ignores zoom, which is acceptable on a TV, but a host "text size" setting that multiplies a `--stage-scale` property would be an accessibility improvement over Jackbox. Radii and outline widths should scale with the space tokens, because a fixed 12 px radius looks tiny on a TV and huge on a phone. A stylelint rule that rejects raw `px` and `vw` literals in font-size, gap, padding and margin keeps new components on the system.

Text fitting should be deterministic rather than clever. Prompts get `text-wrap: balance`, which evens lines for blocks of six or fewer in Chromium, and multi-line answers get `text-wrap: pretty` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/text-wrap)). Character-count buckets (`data-len="s|m|l"`) step the size down for long prompts and switch to Inter at the longest bucket. Fit-to-width tricks are reserved for single-line names and stamps ([kizu.dev](https://kizu.dev/fit-to-width/)). Cards in a head-to-head share the font size of the longer answer's bucket, so one card never shouts louder than its rival, which is a fairness issue as well as an aesthetic one. Layout follows answer count: two equal columns for duels, three columns for list duels, 2x2 for four answers, and a uniform `repeat(auto-fit, minmax(28cqi, 1fr))` grid for five to eight, never mixing card sizes among voting options. Phones-only mode switches on `@container stage (aspect-ratio < 1)` rather than viewport media queries.

### Reveals are a sequence of held beats

Comedy reveals need time to land. Jackbox treats its games as a TV hybrid with deliberate narrative timing ([Built In](https://builtin.com/media-gaming/jackbox-games-design-party-pack)), Fibbage reveals the lies one at a time with their victims and saves the truth for last ([TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/fibbage-review)), and the PP10 team's cautionary bug, in which Tee K.O. 2 "played EVERY SINGLE ANIMATION that was possible at the same time," shows what simultaneous motion does to readability ([Jackbox blog, PP10 directing](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/behind-the-scenes-of-pp10-directing)). The rule for iatrome is that **nothing new starts moving until the previous beat has settled and held for 400-600 ms**, driven by the server's phase clock so the stage and every phone stay in sync. The timeline below is a set of starting values to tune, not a sourced spec. Its total sits inside the 30-45 seconds per matchup that a 15-minute, 16-matchup Quiplash implies. Grand Rounds' current constants (4 s present, 15 s vote, 6.5 s results) already fit that budget.

| Beat | Motion | Hold |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt alone | 400 ms rise and fade (`--ease-out`) | 1.2-1.8 s, or read-aloud length |
| Answer A, then B | Spring drop-in, 450 ms each, 600 ms gap so the room reacts to A first | Until voting opens |
| Voting | Cards still; only the timer and voter-count chips move | Vote window, ending early when complete |
| Lock and tally | 150 ms squash; voter avatars pop under cards at 120-180 ms stagger | 500 ms |
| Attribution | Author names slide in (the "who wrote that?" laugh) | 1.5 s |
| Points | Count-up with tabular numerals, scale pop at the end | 1 s |
| Unanimous | Loser dims to 0.45 opacity; stamp slams from 1.3x to 1x with a 6-10 degree tilt; sound cue | 2 s |
| Jinx or tie | Deadpan treatment: a flat stamp and a small wobble, no confetti | 1.5 s |

Authors stay anonymous until votes lock, because that delay produces the reveal's third laugh. On the web, animate only `transform` and `opacity`, and never animate `box-shadow`, `filter: blur()` or `backdrop-filter`. web.dev warns that shadows and blurs are expensive to paint and that `will-change` should be used sparingly ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/animations-guide)). Spotlighting should therefore use an opacity-animated scrim plus `scale(1.04)` on the focused card, and a growing shadow should be a cross-fade between two pre-rendered shadow layers. Add duration tokens (`--dur-quick 150ms`, `--dur-base 300ms`, `--dur-enter 450ms`, `--beat 600ms`, `--hold 1500ms`) beside the existing `--ease-out` and `--ease-spring`. Reduced motion should honor both `prefers-reduced-motion`, which flags scaling and panning as vestibular triggers ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)), and a host toggle like the one Jackbox ships in Quiplash 3 ([Jackbox PP7 accessibility](https://www.jackboxgames.com/new-accessibility-features-in-the-jackbox-party-pack-7/)). Reduced mode swaps slams and slides for cross-fades but keeps every beat and hold, because pacing is the comedy and motion is only its delivery. Jackbox also offers Extended Timers and No Timers ([Jackbox PP10 accessibility](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/accessibility-features-in-the-jackbox-party-pack-10)), and iatrome's extended-timer setting should scale every mode, not just Grand Rounds.

## Each mode needs a dread beat, a finale and a running gag

The commercial pattern across party games is short sessions, a structural escalation near the end that makes comebacks mathematically possible, and every player acting every round. Trivia Murder Party 3 cut the game from nine questions plus a tiebreak to seven rounds ([Wikipedia: Trivia Murder Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivia_Murder_Party)). Pictionary doubles and then triples points by round ([Netflix Pictionary FAQ](https://games-netflix.helpshift.com/hc/en/43-pictionary/faq/1786-how-do-i-get-points-and-how-is-the-score-calculated-in-classic-mode)). Wavelength's main criticism is downtime ([Wikipedia: Wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength_(game))). End-screen awards should be deadpan rather than merit-based, and harsh loss animations can feel "incredibly discouraging" ([Mechanics of Magic](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2025/04/11/critplay1-amaru/)), so losses should read as gags. The current-state descriptions below come from iatrome's `packages/game/src/constants.ts` and content packs.

| Mode | Biggest gap today | Highest-leverage change |
|---|---|---|
| Grand Rounds | All-play finale crowds at 6-8 players; no per-session frame cap | Count-adaptive Differential; frame budget in the draw; shared card system |
| Sketch | Single-noun terms; drawer paid the same for easy and hard | Compound scene prompts; visible difficulty multipliers; last lap at 2x |
| Survive the Shift | Elimination is silent; ghosts can't win; up to 20 questions | Wrong-answer minigame; final "Sign-out" chase ghosts can win; 10-12 questions |
| Pick Your Poison | Flat 100 for picking a side; majority idle during defence | Graded split prediction; persistent minority marker; majority bets on the defence |
| Didactics | No wager; reveal shows the answer before the room's distribution | Once-per-half Double Down; distribution-first reveal; comic callouts |

### Grand Rounds: protect the duel, cap the frames

Grand Rounds is closest to Jackbox quality already. It has share-based scoring, a 250 consensus bonus, safety quips (curbside answers), an audience block capped at one-third, and a solo-voter cap of 65%. The remaining work is mostly presentation and curation. Apply the beat table above, with anonymous answers until lock. Enforce the structure budget per session. Run every new quip through the persona and jinx tests. Make the finale count-adaptive as described. The `{player}` prompts are the inside-joke category Ham says only works when the joke is collective, and their pack header already limits them to "affectionate roasts only: habits, quirks and choices," which is the right rule. Cap them at one or two per round so a quiet player isn't the subject three times. The Differential pack's escalating list format ("write for an escalating list where the third item lands the joke") is exactly the rule-of-three Thriplash invites, and it should be kept.

### Sketch: pay the drawer for ambition, rescue the stuck

Beyond replacing flat nouns with compound scenes, Sketch should copy Pictionary's visible risk-reward. Netflix's version pays the drawer more for harder words and faster guesses ([Netflix Pictionary FAQ](https://games-netflix.helpshift.com/hc/en/43-pictionary/faq/1786-how-do-i-get-points-and-how-is-the-score-calculated-in-classic-mode)). Showing "Easy x1 / Medium x1.5 / Hard x2" on the choice screen turns the existing `difficulty` field into a brag-or-bust decision. Apply 2x to the last lap so late comebacks exist. Add a "no letters" reminder plus a guesser-side "they wrote it" flag that zeroes the drawer's share if most guessers tap it, which is the skribbl.io critique's own suggestion of letting guessers police the drawer ([Mechanics of Magic](https://mechanicsofmagic.com/2024/04/24/critical-play-scribbl-io-2/)). If nobody has guessed by the 70% hint, let the drawer buy a category reveal ("it's a sign," "it's a drug") for half their share, which spares them 80 seconds of public failure. Stream reactions to the drawer live so a struggling artist gets laughs instead of silence. Add a private "close" signal for near-miss guesses, since `matchGuess` could support edit distance. At 10 players a single lap of 80-second turns runs about 16 minutes, so cap drawers per game or shorten draw time at high counts. A Drawful-style "Differential Diagnosis" round, in which players write fake titles for a drawing and vote among them, would make bad art score. It is worth building as an occasional variant because it gives non-artists a writing path to points.

### Survive the Shift: give elimination a scene and ghosts a door

Trivia Murder Party is tense because a wrong answer does not kill you directly. It sends you to a short, random, often non-trivia minigame where death is possible but not certain, and the dead remain eligible to win through a final race in which **the leader sees fewer answer options** ([Wikipedia: Trivia Murder Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivia_Murder_Party)). Its tone aims for "cute and gruesome," ideas that "earn a laugh or a gasp" ([Jackbox blog, TMP3 Dev Diary](https://jackboxgames.com/blog/trivia-murder-party-3-dev-diary-4)). Survive currently subtracts a coffee on a wrong answer, forgives a mass miss under its "mercy" rule, scores ghosts at 0.5x, and allows a single silent revive after three correct answers in a row. The ghosts' 0.5x reads as permanent second class because no path to victory exists. Four changes fix this. First, insert a dread beat: wrong-answer players go to a five-second "M&M" minigame (a luck pick among three vials, a quick sort, or a one-line excuse to the attending that the room votes on), and only the losers spill their coffee. This gives non-medical players a non-trivia way to survive. Second, replace the tail of the game with a "Sign-out" chase. Everyone races along a hallway track on pick-all-that-apply questions, the leader sees fewer options, ghosts start behind but can win outright, and the first to the exit wins. Third, make the revive a public event, and consider replacing "mercy" with "Last Call," under which the lowest scorer pays when every survivor misses. That trades a protective rule for a dramatic one, and it is a judgment call best settled in playtests. Fourth, cut to about 10-12 questions plus the chase, following TMP's own shortening. HQ Trivia's scarce second life is a model for visibility: show "2 players are on their last coffee" ([Wikipedia: HQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HQ_(video_game))). Play every coffee loss as a gag (a spilled cup, a flat Beep line), never as a red X. HQ's sawtooth difficulty, easy wins scattered among obscure surprises, felt less like a test than a steady climb ([TIME](https://time.com/5189381/hq-trivia-app-questions/)).

### Pick Your Poison: score the read, stigmatize the loner

The strongest predict-the-room games score how well you read other people, keep the non-active side acting, and make being the odd one out a visible but sheddable stigma. Wavelength's opposing team bets left or right of the guess ([Wikipedia: Wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength_(game))). Herd Mentality's Pink Cow holder cannot win until someone else becomes the odd one out ([George Fox University blog](https://blogs.georgefox.edu/dlgp/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/36906)). Guesspionage has players call higher or lower on a percentage ([Jackbox: Guesspionage](https://jackboxgames.com/guesspionage)). Poison pays a flat 100 for predicting the majority side, so a 51/49 split scores the same as 90/10, even though close splits make the funniest reveals. Grade the prediction with a "how lopsided?" call in 25% buckets. Show `minorityCount` live as a persistent marker ("Atypical Presentation") that sits on the most recent lone dissenter until someone else is alone. Spotlight a single dissenter automatically, with a small "Against Medical Advice" bonus and the first defence. During the defence, give the majority a bet ("will this convince anyone?") alongside the existing Convinced tap. Stage the defence big on the TV with the defender's avatar and a reaction burst, because the comedy is in the performance. Make the final dilemma a finale: majority prediction worth triple and the lone dissenter defending automatically. Dilemma quality is measurable from iatrome's own data. Both options should be specific and equally bad ("take every call on Christmas" vs "present at M&M weekly for a year"), and dilemmas that consistently split worse than about 80/20 should be retired, using real pick rates the way Guesspionage uses survey data.

### Didactics: let teams huddle, then reveal the room before the answer

Didactics already scores fairly across team sizes by splitting a 1,000-point pool by the share of each team that answered correctly. It also offers a change of heart, standings every three questions, a double-value final three and an individual MVP. It lacks a betting layer and reveal theatre. Kahoot's own evolution is instructive. Its standard scoring pays nothing extra for streaks and moves that value into an opt-in "Confidence" bet, where a boosted right answer gains and a boosted wrong answer loses the same amount ([Kahoot support](https://support.kahoot.com/hc/en-us/articles/32200674639261)). A once-per-half team "Double Down," which doubles the pool if right and forfeits it if wrong, creates the huddles that team trivia runs on. At 4-40 players the reveal is the show. Bars of the answer distribution should fill first, then the correct answer appears, then a team ticker ("Ortho 7/8, Psych 2/9"), then one deadpan callout ("3 people picked leeches"). Kahoot hosts have asked for an option to remove speed scoring because it punishes careful readers ([Kahoot community](https://support.kahoot.com/hc/en-us/community/posts/38794659972755-Add-option-to-remove-points-awarded-scaling-with-answer-speed)). For long Pro vignettes, a flat bonus for answering in the first half of the timer is fairer than linear decay. Joke distractors, BMJ-Christmas-style findings and historical quackery keep trivia from feeling like a board exam. The evidence base on what makes trivia funny rather than test-like remains thin, so this mode needs the most playtest measurement.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "Jackbox quality" means. It is less a matter of production value than of restraint applied consistently. The prompt leaves the joke to the player. The stage shows one thing at a time and holds it. The game gives the loser a gag and the dead a door. Jackbox's documented failures (stale prompts, a finale that tested the wrong skill, moderation added late, animations that all fired at once) are all failures of that restraint, and iatrome can avoid them from the start because it is still small enough to impose one system across five modes. The same restraint resolves the two-audience problem. Content aimed at institutions, objects and clinicians' own egos is the only material that is funny to both a nurse and her uncle, so the guardrail and the growth strategy are the same rule.

Two things remain unknown and should drive the next round of work. Nobody has measured how laypeople react to clinician humor, and Jackbox's reveal timings exist only in its builds and in gameplay video. Both are cheaply answerable. Record a few fresh-group sessions per mode and log time-to-first-laugh, per-prompt jinx rates and answer diversity, and dilemma split rates, then let those numbers retire weak prompts and tune the beat clock. Because "you can only test a joke once" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/exploring-what-it-takes-to-make-a-funny-game)), those sessions need new players every time, and the development team should not be among them.
